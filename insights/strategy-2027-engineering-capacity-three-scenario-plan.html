Planning Engineering Capacity for 2027: A Three-Scenario Method for CTOs

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

Most engineering plans for the coming year are a single number: the headcount the company expects to reach by December, broken down by team. That format made sense when the main uncertainty was recruiting speed. For 2027 it is fragile. Engineering leaders in Germany, Austria and Switzerland face a cluster of fixed regulatory deadlines, a labour market that has cooled cyclically but remains structurally tight, and a funding environment where a single round or customer contract can change the plan overnight. A three-scenario plan with explicit triggers handles this better than a point estimate, and it gives the board a clear view of what changes when.

The fixed points of 2027

Some parts of the 2027 picture are not scenarios but deadlines. They create demand for engineering work regardless of the economy.

  • EU Cyber Resilience Act. Reporting obligations for actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents have applied since 11 September 2026. The main obligations for products with digital elements apply from 11 December 2027, which means secure development processes, vulnerability handling and documentation must be in place for products placed on the market from that date.
  • EU AI Act. Under the Digital Omnibus agreement reached in May 2026, obligations for stand-alone high-risk AI systems listed in Annex III move to 2 December 2027, and obligations for AI embedded in regulated products under Annex I move to 2 August 2028. Companies building such systems have more time, but the engineering work for risk management, logging and documentation still has to be scheduled.
  • SAP ERP 6.0. Mainstream maintenance for SAP ECC ends at the end of 2027. Many Mittelstand firms will compete for the same migration specialists during the year.

These deadlines fall into the second half of 2027. Work that has to be finished by then needs to start in the first half, which means capacity decisions are due now.

The variable inputs

The labour market is the first variable. Bitkom's September 2026 study counts 79.000 missing IT specialists in Germany, down from 149.000 in 2023, but 76 percent of companies expect the shortage to worsen again. Labour costs are the second: the Hans-Böckler-Stiftung's IMK reported German private-sector hourly labour costs of EUR 45,70 for 2025, an increase of 3,4 percent. Funding is the third: KfW Research counted EUR 9,5 billion raised by German startups in the first nine months of 2026, more than in any full year since 2022, but concentrated in fewer deals. Each of these can move materially within a year.

The three-scenario method

  1. Define the base case. Start from committed roadmap items and the fixed regulatory work above. Estimate the engineering capacity required per quarter, split by skill family: product engineering, platform and infrastructure, security and compliance engineering, data and ML, and specialist areas such as embedded or SAP.
  2. Define an upside case. Typical upside triggers are a new funding round, a large customer contract, or a product line that gains traction faster than expected. Estimate the additional capacity and the date by which it would be needed.
  3. Define a downside case. Typical triggers are a delayed round, the loss of a key customer, or a deliberate slowdown to extend runway. Estimate how much capacity could be released and how quickly.
  4. Attach a trigger to each scenario. A trigger is an observable event with a date, such as a signed term sheet, a contract above a defined value, or revenue below plan for two consecutive months.
  5. Map capacity sources to scenarios. Decide in advance which capacity you would add or release in each case, and through which channel.

A worked structure

The structure makes one principle visible: regulatory work is protected in every scenario, because missing a fixed deadline costs more than any saving. Flexibility comes from supporting work and from the choice of channel.

Matching capacity channels to scenario risk

Internal hires are the right choice for capacity that is needed in all three scenarios. They take longest to add and are hardest to remove, so they should cover the floor of the plan. External capacity with a defined notice period is the natural fit for the difference between base and upside, and for work that can be paused in a downside case. Fixed regulatory work often fits a bounded external work package well, because scope and deadline are known.

Two questions help test the plan. If the upside trigger fires in March, can you add capacity by May? If the downside trigger fires in June, can you reduce cost by September without losing core knowledge? If the answer to either is no, the channel mix needs adjustment.

Presenting the plan

Show the board one chart with three capacity lines over four quarters, a table of triggers, and the share of each scenario covered by flexible capacity. Review the triggers monthly in the leadership team and quarterly with the board. This replaces the annual debate about a single headcount number with a standing agreement on how the company will respond.
