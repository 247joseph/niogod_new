Architecture Decision Records for Remote Engineering Pods: A Working Standard

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

When an external team builds part of your product, the code arrives in your repository. The reasoning behind it often does not. Six months later, nobody remembers why the pod chose an event queue over direct calls, or why a library was rejected. For a DACH company that intends to own its system long term, this is a real exposure. The answer is a lightweight, enforced practice of architecture decision records (ADRs), written by the pod, reviewed by the client and stored next to the code.

Why decisions get lost in distributed delivery

In a co-located team, reasoning spreads through conversation. In a distributed setup, conversations happen in a limited overlap window, in calls that are not recorded, and in chat threads that scroll away. The broader data on knowledge loss is sobering. In the Stack Overflow Developer Survey 2024, fewer than half of developers said they can easily find up-to-date information inside their organisation, and three in four had answered questions they had already answered before. Atlassian's State of Developer Experience Report 2025 named locating information, including services, documentation and APIs, as the leading source of friction for developers.

Demographics add pressure. Destatis reported in September 2025 that about 13.4 million people in the German labour force, roughly 31 percent, will have passed the statutory retirement age of 67 by 2039. Many DACH engineering organisations will see experienced architects leave in the coming decade. Decisions that live only in their heads leave with them.

The format: keep it short

Michael Nygard proposed the ADR format in a blog post on 15 November 2011, recommending one to two pages per decision with five sections: title, context, decision, status and consequences. That format still works, and we would resist the temptation to extend it. A pod-friendly template looks like this:

SectionContentTypical length
TitleA short noun phrase, numbered, for example "ADR 014: Message broker for telemetry ingestion"One line
StatusProposed, accepted, superseded by ADR n, or deprecatedOne line
ContextThe forces at play: requirements, constraints, client policies, cost, team skillsOne to three paragraphs
Options consideredTwo to four options with the main trade-off of eachA short list
DecisionWhat was chosen, in active voiceOne paragraph
ConsequencesWhat becomes easier, what becomes harder, and what must be revisitedA short list
ApprovalClient architect or CTO who accepted it, with dateOne line

The "options considered" and "approval" rows are additions we recommend for external delivery. The first prevents the record from becoming a justification after the fact. The second documents that the client, not the vendor, owns the architectural direction.

When an ADR is required

Not every choice needs a record. Agree on explicit triggers during onboarding. A practical list:

  • A new runtime dependency, framework or managed cloud service.
  • A change to a public interface, data schema or event contract.
  • A choice that affects security, data residency or personal data processing.
  • A decision with a cost impact above a threshold the client sets.
  • Any decision that reverses or supersedes an earlier ADR.
  • Any decision the pod expects the client's own team to inherit.

If a pull request introduces one of these without an ADR reference, the reviewer sends it back. That single rule does more than any documentation policy.

Where the records live

Store ADRs as Markdown files in the repository, in a folder such as docs/adr, numbered sequentially. This keeps them versioned with the code, visible in pull requests and available to anyone who clones the repository, including your future in-house team. Avoid putting them in a vendor-controlled wiki. If the engagement ends, the records must stay where the code is.

The review loop

  1. The pod's Senior Consultant drafts the ADR with status "proposed" and opens a pull request.
  2. The client's architect or CTO reviews it asynchronously, within an agreed period, typically two working days.
  3. Open points are resolved in the weekly architecture session within the overlap window.
  4. The client approves, the status changes to "accepted", and implementation proceeds.
  5. When a decision is later replaced, the old record is not edited. A new ADR supersedes it, and both remain.

This sequence also clarifies responsibility. The pod proposes and implements within an agreed scope. The client decides on direction. That separation is useful operationally, and it fits the structure of a delivery contract in which the vendor owns how the work is done but the client owns what the system should become.

What the client has to contribute

The practice fails if only the pod invests in it. The client side needs to name one person with authority to accept or reject ADRs, usually the CTO in a scaleup or the lead architect in a Mittelstand IT department. That person commits to the review period and attends the weekly architecture session. The client should also supply the constraints the pod cannot know: group IT policies, preferred cloud regions, licensing restrictions, and any regulatory requirements such as medical device or export control rules that narrow the options. Recording these once, in an "ADR 000: Constraints and principles" file, saves repeated explanation and gives every later record a shared starting point.

Signs that the practice is working

  • New engineers, on either side, read the ADR folder in their first week and ask fewer "why" questions.
  • Architecture discussions reference ADR numbers rather than memories of calls.
  • Superseded records exist, which shows decisions are being revisited honestly.
  • The client's team can explain the system's main design choices without the pod in the room.

Common mistakes

Writing ADRs retroactively in bulk before a handover produces documents that describe outcomes, not reasoning. Writing them too long turns them into design specifications nobody maintains. Letting the vendor approve its own decisions removes the control the practice is meant to provide. And keeping them outside the repository guarantees they drift out of date.

Architecture decision records cost a pod perhaps an hour per significant decision. They return that time many times over at every onboarding, audit and handover. In a niogod™ pod, the Senior Consultant owns the ADR log as part of the sprint deliverables, and each record is submitted for your approval rather than filed for the vendor's convenience. If your system will outlive any single team that works on it, this is a habit worth requiring from the first sprint.
