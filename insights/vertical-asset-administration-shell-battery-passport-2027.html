Battery Passport 2027: Building It on the Asset Administration Shell

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

From 18 February 2027, industrial batteries above 2 kWh, electric vehicle batteries and batteries for light means of transport placed on the EU market need a digital battery passport. For German machine builders and component suppliers, this is the first binding Digital Product Passport, and it will not be the last. The efficient response is not a one-off passport project but an Asset Administration Shell based data layer that serves the battery passport now and the product passports that follow under the Ecodesign for Sustainable Products Regulation (ESPR).

The timeline that drives the work

According to BSI Group's overview of the EU Batteries Regulation, the battery passport becomes mandatory on 18 February 2027 for batteries placed on the market or put into service in the EU. It is a digital record per battery, accessed through a QR code linked to a unique identifier, and covers information on manufacturing and chemistry, material composition and responsible sourcing, carbon footprint and recycled content, performance and state of health, and repair, disassembly and end of life. Some data is public, some restricted to authorised parties. The economic operator placing the battery on the market is responsible for keeping it accurate and up to date.

The broader ESPR framework is following. The EU Digital Product Passport registry has been operational since 20 July 2026, product-specific requirements for iron and steel are targeted for the fourth quarter of 2026, and delegated acts for textiles, aluminium and tyres are planned for 2027. Every manufacturer that sells physical products into the EU should expect passport obligations to reach its portfolio within a few years.

The data problem behind the passport

The passport itself is a thin layer. The hard part is the data behind it, and German industry is candid about the gap. In Bitkom's May 2026 survey of 555 manufacturing companies, 62 percent rated digital twins as very or fairly important for competitiveness, yet 60 percent described their own company as a laggard. Asked why implementations fail, 57 percent said the required data was missing and 48 percent pointed to deficits in IT infrastructure. A battery passport exposes those gaps with a legal deadline attached.

Why the Asset Administration Shell is the right foundation

The Asset Administration Shell (AAS), maintained by the Industrial Digital Twin Association, is the standardised digital twin model of Industrie 4.0. It describes an asset through submodels, each a structured, versioned set of properties with semantic identifiers. That structure fits the passport problem well:

  • One source, many views. The same submodel can feed the public passport view, the restricted view for authorities and recyclers, and internal service applications.
  • Semantic identifiers. Properties linked to established dictionaries reduce mapping work with customers and suppliers.
  • Supplier data exchange. Cell suppliers, pack assemblers and machine builders can exchange submodels rather than spreadsheets, which matters because carbon footprint and material data originate upstream.
  • Reuse for ESPR. When further product groups follow, new submodels are added to an existing infrastructure instead of starting another project.

Implementation plan in six steps

  1. Scope. List every product that contains an in-scope battery, who places it on the market and in which configuration. Clarify roles with suppliers in writing.
  2. Data inventory. Map each required passport field to its system of record: ERP, PLM, MES, supplier declarations or calculations. Mark fields with no current source.
  3. Submodel design. Use existing AAS submodel templates where available, define company-specific submodels where not, and agree semantic identifiers early.
  4. Integration. Build connectors from ERP, PLM and MES into an AAS repository, with validation rules that reject incomplete records before they reach a passport.
  5. Access and identifiers. Implement the unique identifier and QR resolution, role-based access for restricted data, and a process for updating dynamic data such as state of health.
  6. Operation. Define ownership per submodel, change control, monitoring for data quality and an archive strategy for the lifetime of the battery.

Governance: who owns the passport after go-live

A passport is a living record, not a document that is generated once. Ownership therefore has to be split clearly. Product management or compliance owns the content definition and the interpretation of the regulation. Engineering owns the data pipeline, validation rules and the repository. Each source system owner remains responsible for the quality of the data it delivers, and suppliers are bound by contract to deliver their parts on time and in an agreed format. A short monthly data quality report, showing incomplete or rejected records per product line, keeps the topic visible after the initial deadline has passed. Without this governance, passports degrade silently, and the first sign of a problem is a request from a market surveillance authority or a recycler that cannot be answered from the system.

Checklist for the next 90 days

  • Inventory of in-scope products and the economic operator role for each
  • Field-by-field mapping to systems of record, with gaps flagged
  • Supplier data requests issued with deadlines and formats
  • Decision on AAS repository and integration architecture
  • Pilot passport for one product generated end to end from source systems
  • Owner named for passport data quality after go-live

This article is general information and not legal advice; the scope of the Batteries Regulation for a specific product should be confirmed with qualified counsel.
