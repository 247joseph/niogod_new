German or English? A Working Language Policy for Mixed Engineering Teams

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

In most German-Indian engineering setups, nobody decides the working language. Code ends up in English, the specification arrives in German, tickets are a mix, and the Abnahme protocol is written in whichever language the person on duty preferred. Each choice is reasonable on its own; together they create friction, translation work and occasional misunderstandings in exactly the documents that carry contractual weight. The solution is a short language policy that decides, artefact by artefact, which language is used and who bridges the gap.

The language picture in numbers

German language skills are a real constraint in the DACH labour market. In Bitkom's 2026 study of the IT labour market, insufficient German was the most frequently cited hiring barrier, named by 47 percent of companies, ahead of insufficient professional qualifications at 43 percent. Many German organisations, especially in the Mittelstand, still run their internal processes, contracts and management communication in German.

At the same time, English is widely available on the German side. Germany ranked 4th out of 123 countries and regions in the EF English Proficiency Index 2025, up from 10th the year before. Most German engineers can work comfortably in English; the question is more often whether product owners, operations staff and management can.

On the Indian side, the talent base is large and English-native in a professional context. NASSCOM put India's technology workforce at about 5.8 million in the 2025 financial year, and GitHub's Octoverse 2025 counted about 21.9 million Indian developers on its platform, with 5.2 million added in a single year. English is the default language of that ecosystem. German is not, which is why the bridge has to be designed rather than assumed.

Decide per artefact

ArtefactRecommended languageReasoning
Source code, identifiers, code commentsEnglishIndustry standard; libraries and tools are in English; future hires can read it
Commit messages, pull request descriptionsEnglishRead by engineers on both sides; searchable
Architecture decision records, technical documentationEnglishEngineering audience; lasting reference
User stories and acceptance criteriaEnglish, with German domain terms preservedMust be understood exactly by the people who build and test
Domain glossaryBilingualAnchors terms such as Auftrag, Lieferschein or Stückliste
User interface textsTarget language of the product, managed as translation filesNever hard-coded; reviewed by a native speaker
Leistungsbeschreibung, contract, Abnahme protocolGerman (or bilingual with German prevailing)Contractual documents under German law; signed by German management
Steering reports, escalationsThe language of the client's managementNuance matters most at this level
Release notes for users and supportThe language of the readersSupport teams in the Mittelstand often work in German

The domain glossary is the most important document

Misunderstandings in mixed teams rarely come from grammar. They come from domain terms that do not translate cleanly. "Auftrag" can mean order, job or commission depending on the system. "Freigabe" can be approval, release or clearance. A bilingual glossary, owned by the client's product owner and maintained by the pod, removes most of this. Good practice:

  • One row per term: the German term, the agreed English term, a one-sentence definition and the system object it maps to.
  • German domain terms may appear untranslated in English stories and code where no precise English equivalent exists, as long as they are in the glossary.
  • New terms are added during refinement, not after a defect has shown that they were understood differently.

Who bridges the gap

Machine translation has made written German accessible to engineers who do not speak it, and pod members should use it freely for reading internal documents. It is not a substitute for a person who understands both the language and the context in the moments that matter: refinement of complex requirements, acceptance discussions, and escalations. Those conversations need someone who can hold them in German, notice when "ja, grundsätzlich" means "no", and carry the result accurately into English stories.

That bridge role is best held by one named person on the vendor side, close to the team, rather than spread across a translation step. It keeps the engineers working in English while the client's people work in German, without either side having to compromise.

Contractual documents

For a Werkvertrag or Dienstvertrag under German law, keep the contract, the Leistungsbeschreibung and the Abnahme protocols in German, or bilingual with a clause stating that the German version prevails. Make sure the acceptance criteria referenced in the German documents are identical in substance to those in the English stories; a simple way is to reference stories by their ticket identifiers rather than restating them.

Common failure patterns

  • German acceptance criteria, English tests. The product owner writes criteria in German, an engineer translates them while writing tests, and the translation silently shifts meaning. Fix: criteria in English, reviewed by the bridge person, glossary terms preserved.
  • Mixed-language code. German identifiers next to English ones make the codebase harder to search and harder for future hires. Fix: English identifiers, German domain terms only where the glossary says so.
  • Escalations in the wrong language. A German managing director receives an English escalation note and misses the urgency. Fix: escalations in the language of the recipient.

Rules of thumb for daily work

  • Meetings with engineers default to English; meetings with business stakeholders default to their language, with the bridge person present.
  • Written questions from the pod to the client may be in English; answers that change scope are confirmed in the ticket in English.
  • No important decision is taken only verbally across a language boundary; it is summarised in writing within the same day.
  • Review the language policy after the first three sprints and adjust where it causes friction.

This article is general information and does not constitute legal advice; the language and precedence of contractual documents should be reviewed by qualified counsel.

A language policy is a small document with a large effect, because it removes friction from exactly the places where accuracy matters.
