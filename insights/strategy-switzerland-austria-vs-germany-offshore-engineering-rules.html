Offshore Engineering in Switzerland and Austria: How Rules Differ from Germany

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

Vendors and buyers often speak of DACH as a single market. For engineering services delivered from India, it is three legal systems with similar vocabulary and different consequences. Germany regulates labour leasing through the AÜG and a permit from the Bundesagentur für Arbeit. Austria has its own Arbeitskräfteüberlassungsgesetz, also abbreviated AÜG, with a statutory substance-over-form test. Switzerland regulates labour leasing through the Arbeitsvermittlungsgesetz (AVG) and, notably, does not permit leasing staff from abroad into Switzerland at all. Data protection differs as well: Germany and Austria apply the DSGVO, Switzerland its revised Federal Act on Data Protection. This briefing summarises the differences that matter when you engage a remote team in India.

The labour markets in figures

All three countries face skills shortages, but the shape differs.

  • Switzerland. ICT-Berufsbildung Schweiz projected in September 2025 that the country will be short of 54,400 ICT specialists by 2033, against a gross additional demand of 128,600. In 2024, 266,000 people worked in ICT occupations. The skills shortage index of the Adecco Group and the University of Zurich fell about 22 percent year on year in 2025, but engineers and comparable technical specialists moved up to third place among the scarcest professions.
  • Austria. In a spring 2025 survey of 2,483 companies conducted by ibw for the Austrian Economic Chamber (WKO), 78 percent reported a shortage of workers, and the study estimates around 176,000 open positions for skilled workers.
  • Cost. Eurostat data reported by Destatis put the average hourly labour cost in 2025 at EUR 46.30 in Austria and EUR 45 in Germany, against an EU average of EUR 34.90.
  • Germany. Bitkom's 2026 labour market study counts 79,000 missing IT specialists.

Demand for external engineering capacity is therefore similar across DACH. The legal frameworks for supplying it are not.

Austria: the statutory substance test

Austrian law makes explicit what German practice derives from case law. Under §4 Abs. 1 of the Austrian AÜG, the true economic substance of an arrangement, not its outward form, decides whether labour leasing exists. §4 Abs. 2 lists indicators that point to leasing even where a contract for work is signed, for example where the contractor does not produce a work that is distinct and attributable to it, where the work is performed mainly with the client's materials and tools, where the workers are organisationally integrated into the client's business and subject to its supervision, or where the contractor does not bear the risk of the result.

Consequences can be significant. If an arrangement is reclassified, the AÜG applies, including equal treatment rules, and the client as Beschäftiger can be liable for wages and social contributions under §14 AÜG. Labour leasing in Austria is a regulated trade. For an offshore engagement, this means the Werkvertrag must be real in practice: a defined, attributable result, vendor-provided tools and methods, vendor-side supervision and genuine result risk.

Switzerland: no leasing from abroad

Switzerland is the strictest of the three on one point. Art. 12 Abs. 2 AVG prohibits Personalverleih from abroad into Switzerland. A foreign vendor therefore cannot lawfully provide staff who work under the instruction of a Swiss client in the manner of leased employees. If an engagement with an Indian team is classified as Personalverleih, there is no permit route to fix it.

The Swiss test focuses on whether essential instruction rights are transferred to the client. The contract label is not decisive. Indicators of leasing include personal, organisational and temporal integration into the client's operations, use of the client's tools, billing by the hour instead of by result, and the client bearing the risk of poor performance. A Werkvertrag under Art. 363 OR, where the result is in the foreground, or a carefully structured Auftrag under Art. 394 OR, are the appropriate frameworks, and the operating model must match them.

Switzerland: data protection under the revised DSG

The revised Swiss Federal Act on Data Protection has applied since 1 September 2023. Cross-border disclosure is governed by Art. 16 DSG. Countries with adequate protection are listed in Annex 1 of the Data Protection Ordinance; India is not on that list. Transfers to India therefore need safeguards, typically standard contractual clauses recognised by the Federal Data Protection and Information Commissioner (FDPIC). The FDPIC has recognised the EU standard contractual clauses, subject to Swiss-specific adaptations, for example on the competent supervisory authority and the applicable law. Note that the revised DSG provides for criminal fines of up to CHF 250,000, imposed on responsible individuals rather than the company.

Practical checklist by country

  • All three: define deliverables and acceptance criteria; keep instruction rights with the vendor's lead; avoid direct task assignment by client staff to individual offshore engineers.
  • Germany: document the Werkvertrag or Dienstvertrag structure; check §13b UStG treatment; agree SCCs and a DSGVO Art. 28 agreement where personal data is processed.
  • Austria: test the arrangement against each §4 Abs. 2 AÜG indicator; ensure the vendor provides its own tools and bears result risk; if engineers travel to Austria for work, check LSD-BG reporting.
  • Switzerland: treat any drift toward leasing as a hard stop, given Art. 12 Abs. 2 AVG; use FDPIC-recognised clauses with Swiss adaptations; account for Bezugsteuer.

Language and expectations

Beyond the law, expectations of collaboration differ too. Swiss clients work in German, French or Italian depending on the region, and in German-speaking Switzerland standard written German is the language of contracts and documentation, while meetings often start in Swiss German. In Austria, some terms in contracts and standards differ from German usage. A project lead who knows these differences avoids misunderstandings in specifications and acceptance records.

One structure, three jurisdictions

Country-specific contract terms, governing law and data transfer clauses need to be adapted for Austrian and Swiss clients, even where the operating model is identical. This briefing is general information, not legal or tax advice.

A delivery model in which the vendor directs its own team and the client accepts results is the common denominator that holds in all three countries. niogod™ builds its pods on that logic, with a German-speaking Projektleiter on the vendor side and a Werkvertrag led structure with Abnahme (see security and compliance). If you operate in more than one DACH country, it is worth settling that structure before the first Leistungsbeschreibung is written.
