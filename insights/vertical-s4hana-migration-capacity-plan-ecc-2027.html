ECC Maintenance Ends 2027: S/4HANA Migration Capacity Planning

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

SAP ECC mainstream maintenance ends at the close of 2027, and a large part of the DACH installed base will not be on S/4HANA by then. For Mittelstand IT leaders, the decisive question is no longer whether to migrate but whether there will be enough qualified engineering capacity to do it in a sensible sequence. The answer is to plan capacity by workstream rather than by headcount, to protect scarce in-house SAP knowledge for the decisions only it can make, and to move well-specified technical work such as custom code remediation, test automation and data validation to a delivery model that can scale.

The deadline and the reality

SAP's mainstream maintenance for ECC ends at the end of 2027, with extended maintenance available to the end of 2030 at an additional premium, as The Register reported in February 2026. The same report covered a survey by the German-speaking SAP user group DSAG among 198 senior leaders, conducted between December 2025 and January 2026: only 37 percent of ECC users planned to complete the switch to S/4HANA by the end of 2027, A small group of 4 percent was even targeting 2033.

Execution risk is high. Research by ISG among 200 senior decision-makers, reported in February 2026, found that nearly 60 percent of SAP migration projects were delayed and over budget. The labour market adds pressure: Bitkom counted 79,000 unfilled IT positions in Germany in its September 2026 study, and 97 percent of companies with IT vacancies reported difficulties filling them. Experienced SAP developers are among the hardest profiles to hire, and every company in the region is looking for them in the same window.

Plan by workstream, not by headcount

A migration consists of workstreams with very different skill and context requirements. Separating them makes it clear where in-house knowledge is indispensable and where well-specified work can be delivered by others.

Target process design and fit-to-standardHighLowIn-house with key users
Custom code analysis and remediationMediumHighExternal team against analysis results

Interface migrationMediumHighExternal team with integration owner in-house
Test automation and regression testingLow to mediumHighExternal team
Cutover planning and hypercareHighMediumIn-house lead, external support

An illustrative capacity calculation

The following is an illustrative calculation, not a benchmark. Assume an ECC system with 4,000 custom objects, of which analysis shows 1,500 still in use and 600 requiring changes for S/4HANA. If remediation averages one person-day per object including test, that is 600 person-days, or roughly three engineers for one year. Add rebuilding 40 extensions in a clean core approach at an assumed 10 person-days each (400 person-days) and test automation for 150 core processes at an assumed 2 person-days each (300 person-days). The technical workload alone is around 1,300 person-days, before process design, data migration and cutover. For a company with five in-house SAP staff who also run daily operations, this demonstrates why the work needs to be either stretched over several years or partly delivered externally.

A sequence that protects scarce knowledge

  1. Usage and custom code analysis first. Retire unused objects before migrating anything. Every object removed is effort saved for good.
  2. Fix target processes with key users. This is the work only your people can do. Free them from technical tasks to do it.
  3. Write remediation and rebuild work as specifications. Each object or extension becomes a ticket with expected behaviour, test cases and acceptance criteria.
  4. Build test automation early. Automated regression tests on ECC become the safety net for the migrated system and for every later release.
  5. Run rehearsal migrations. At least two full trial migrations with data validation reports before the productive cutover.
  6. Plan hypercare capacity. The weeks after go-live need more people than the weeks before. Agree that capacity in advance.

Keeping knowledge in-house

The most common objection to external delivery in SAP programmes is the fear of losing knowledge. It is a valid concern, and it is addressed through structure rather than proximity. Every package delivered externally should come with updated technical documentation, test cases that live in your repository and a short handover session recorded for the internal team. Architecture decisions, naming conventions and the clean core rules remain owned by an internal lead who reviews every package before acceptance. Under these conditions, knowledge accumulates in your systems and your documentation, not in the heads of individual contractors.

Contract structure for migration packages

Packages with clear expected results suit a Werkvertrag under BGB §631, with acceptance (Abnahme) under BGB §640 against the agreed test cases. The external team organises its own work and is not integrated into your line organisation or subject to day-to-day instructions from your staff, which keeps the arrangement outside the scope of temporary agency work under §1 AÜG. Work that cannot be specified in advance, such as open-ended support during hypercare, needs a different structure and careful drafting.

Questions for the steering committee

  • Which deadline is realistic given our capacity: 2027, or 2030 with extended maintenance?
  • How many in-house SAP staff days per month are actually available after operations?
  • Which workstreams can be specified clearly enough to be delivered as work packages?
  • Who signs off each package, and against which acceptance criteria?
  • How do we keep knowledge in-house when external teams deliver part of the work?

This article is general information and not legal advice; contract design should be reviewed by qualified counsel.
