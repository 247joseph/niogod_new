Remote Access to HIL Test Rigs: A Setup for Distributed Robotics Teams

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

In robotics and automation companies, the hardware-in-the-loop rig is where software stops being theoretical. It is also where distributed teams most often break down: remote engineers can simulate all day, but the controller, drive or sensor board they need to test against sits in a lab in Stuttgart or Linz, booked by whoever walked in first. The answer is not to ship rigs abroad or to fly people in. It is to treat the rig as a shared, remotely operable service with a booking model, a defined safety envelope and complete telemetry, so that an engineer in Bangalore can run a test at 08:00 their time while the German lab is still dark.

Why rig time has become the constraint

Demand for automation software keeps growing. The International Federation of Robotics reported in September 2026 that Germany installed 24.842 industrial robots in 2025, which was 41 percent of all EU installations. Each new product generation adds controller variants, safety functions and integration tests, and each of those needs time on real hardware.

People are scarcer than rigs. Bitkom's September 2026 study counts 79.000 unfilled IT positions in Germany and found that 97 percent of companies with IT vacancies struggle to fill them. Testing is explicitly part of the gap: 6 percent of companies with open roles are searching for QA and test profiles, and only 9 percent already use AI in software testing. Meanwhile India's technology workforce reached about 5,8 million people in FY25 according to NASSCOM's Strategic Review 2025, which makes it realistic to add test and embedded capacity there, provided those engineers can reach the hardware.

The four layers of a remotely operable rig

1. Physical safety envelope

The rig must be safe to operate with nobody in the room. That means mechanical limits and guarding sized for the worst-case command, a hardware emergency stop that the local lab controls, and a watchdog that drops the rig into a safe state if the network link or the test runner stops sending heartbeats. Anything with real kinetic energy, such as a full manipulator or a mobile platform, needs either a fenced cell or a reduced-energy configuration for unattended runs. Remote operation never replaces the risk assessment; it adds one scenario to it.

2. Power and reset control

Most remote HIL failures are banal: a board hangs and nobody can power-cycle it. Network-switched power distribution units, a debug probe permanently attached, and a serial console server solve 80 percent of the problems without a person walking over. Document the recovery sequence and make it scriptable.

3. Access and scheduling

Access goes through a jump host or zero-trust gateway with named accounts, not through a shared VPN profile. A booking calendar with slots of one to four hours, visible to both sites, prevents silent conflicts. Long regression runs go into overnight slots in the German time zone, which are working hours in India.

4. Telemetry and recording

Every run records the firmware hash, test parameters, the full log, bus traffic (CAN, EtherCAT or similar) and, where relevant, a camera view of the rig. Without this, a failed run at 06:00 becomes a debate rather than a defect report.

Organising the work around the rig

A rig shared across two time zones changes how sprints are planned. Three practices make it work.

  • Test plans before rig time. No one books a slot without a written test intent and expected outcome. This also produces the evidence that later supports acceptance of a sprint.
  • A rig owner on each side. In Germany, a lab engineer owns the physical state and hardware revisions. On the remote side, one engineer owns the test runner, scripts and booking hygiene.
  • Hardware revision control. Every board swap is logged like a code change. Most cross-site disputes come from tests that ran on different hardware than everyone assumed.

Checklist before opening the rig to a remote team

  1. Risk assessment updated for unattended and remote operation, signed off locally.
  2. Hardware emergency stop and network watchdog tested.
  3. Switched power, console server and debug probe installed and scripted.
  4. Named access through a gateway, with session logging.
  5. Booking calendar shared, overnight regression slots defined.
  6. Run recording: firmware hash, parameters, logs, bus traces, camera.
  7. Hardware revision log maintained by the lab owner.

What this buys you

An illustrative calculation: a rig used eight hours a day by a single site becomes available for up to 16 productive hours across two time zones, plus unattended overnight regression. For a team whose release cadence is limited by integration testing, that is often the cheapest capacity increase available, cheaper than a second rig and much cheaper than waiting for an additional local hire.

Common mistakes at the start

Three mistakes are easy to make. First, the rig is opened before recovery is scripted, so the remote team loses its slot at night because nobody can be called. Second, bookings are not linked to the sprint plan, and important tests compete for the same slots in the last week of a sprint. Third, there is no clear rule on who may abort a hanging run. All three can be settled in advance with little effort and belong in the rig's operating manual, which both sides know and maintain.
