MDR Rule 11 for Software Startups: Classification as a Roadmap Decision

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

For a digital health or medtech software startup, the most expensive line in the product strategy is often a single sentence: the intended purpose. Under Rule 11 of the EU Medical Device Regulation (MDR), that sentence largely determines the risk class, and the risk class determines whether a notified body is involved, how much clinical evidence is needed and how long it takes before the first euro of revenue. The practical answer is to treat classification as an architecture and roadmap decision made early and deliberately, not as a regulatory formality handled after the product is built.

How Rule 11 works

Rule 11 in Annex VIII of the MDR classifies software in three steps. Software intended to provide information used to take decisions with diagnostic or therapeutic purposes is class IIa, unless those decisions could cause death or an irreversible deterioration of health (class III) or a serious deterioration of health or a surgical intervention (class IIb). Software intended to monitor physiological processes is class IIa, or class IIb where it monitors vital physiological parameters and variations could result in immediate danger to the patient. All other software is class I. In practice, very little software with a genuine medical purpose stays in class I, and every class above I requires a notified body.

The guidance has become stricter, not looser

The Medical Device Coordination Group published revision 1 of MDCG 2019-11 on 17 June 2025. As summarised by Emergo by UL, the revision introduces the term medical device artificial intelligence (MDAI), clarifies that AI-enabled medical device software is also subject to the AI Act, requires a clear intended purpose for each module of modular software, and adds references to software that informs clinical prognosis or prediction. The expanded examples are expected to place more software in higher classes rather than fewer.

Capacity at notified bodies is the second constraint. A Team-NB survey reported by RAPS in May 2026 counted 16,453 MDR and IVDR certificates issued in 2025, about 45 percent more than in 2024, but noted that reviews typically take 13 to 18 months. On top of this, the transitional periods for legacy devices under Regulation 2023/607 run until 31 December 2027 for class III and implantable class IIb devices and until 31 December 2028 for the others, which keeps notified bodies busy with recertifications.

Reform is under way but not yet in force. The European Commission proposed a targeted revision of the MDR and IVDR on 16 December 2025, including the removal of the five-year maximum validity of certificates and the option to agree a predetermined change control plan with the notified body for specific future changes. For software teams, a change control plan would be valuable, but planning should be based on the rules that apply today.

A sequence for the first classification

  1. Write the intended purpose as a product requirement. Users, setting, condition, the decision the output informs and what the software explicitly does not do.
  2. Map every feature on the roadmap against Rule 11. Mark features that would change the class. These need a separate regulatory decision before development starts.
  3. Decide the module boundary. Analytics, administration and patient engagement features often do not need to be part of the medical device. A clean boundary keeps them on a faster release cycle.
  4. Check AI Act exposure. If the device uses AI and requires a notified body, it falls under Annex I of the AI Act, with obligations applying from 2 August 2028 after the Digital Omnibus.
  5. Book notified body capacity early. With review times of 13 to 18 months, the conversation should start well before the technical documentation is complete.
  6. Align marketing, sales and instructions for use. A sales deck that promises more than the intended purpose can undermine the classification.

Illustrative timeline for a class IIa product

As an illustrative example, not a forecast: a startup that fixes its intended purpose and module boundary in the first quarter, builds the quality management system and the first certifiable release over the following three to four quarters, and submits to a notified body afterwards, should plan with a further 13 to 18 months of review based on the Team-NB figures. Market entry is therefore roughly two and a half to three years after the classification decision. Every feature that later moves the product to IIb restarts part of that clock. This is why the roadmap mapping in step two matters more than any single sprint.

Common mistakes

  • Classifying on the first release only, then discovering that the second release moves to IIb
  • Mixing medical and non-medical code in one deployable unit, which puts every change under design control
  • Treating "decision support" as automatically lower risk, regardless of the condition
  • Assuming the December 2025 reform proposal already applies

This article is general information and not regulatory or legal advice; the classification of a specific device should be confirmed with qualified regulatory experts and, where applicable, the notified body.
