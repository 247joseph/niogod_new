Building a Fremdpersonal Control System for External Engineers

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

In most German scaleups, external engineers arrive through three doors at once: engineering managers sign freelancers, procurement contracts agencies and founders bring in trusted contacts. Nobody owns the overall picture, and that is precisely the picture an auditor asks for. A Fremdpersonal control system closes this gap. It is not a large compliance programme but a small set of gates, a register and clear roles that make every external engagement visible, classified and reviewed. Built once, it turns status and AÜG risk from a surprise into a managed variable.

Why the pressure is rising

Demand for external capacity remains structural. Bitkom's 2026 labour market study estimates that about 79.000 IT specialists are still missing in Germany, down from 149.000 in 2023, and 97 percent of companies with IT vacancies report difficulty filling them. External teams and freelancers fill much of that gap.

At the same time, enforcement is becoming data-driven. The Deutsche Rentenversicherung carries out about 400.000 employer audits per year and is developing KIRA, an AI tool to flag anomalies in employer data. Since 1 January 2026, customs (FKS) may use automated data analysis and AI for risk-based audit selection, and in 2025 it checked about 25.800 employers. Companies that cannot quickly produce a consistent overview of their external staff will struggle when either authority asks.

The status question remains genuinely uncertain. In 2024, the DRV Clearingstelle handled 23.052 status determination procedures, according to a Bundestag reply, and in the first half of 2025 around 40 percent of classified decisions found dependent employment.

The four components

1. A single register

Every external person or team with access to your systems or working on your products appears in one register, regardless of who signed the contract. Minimum fields:

  • Vendor (legal entity or individual), contract type (Werkvertrag, Dienstvertrag, ANÜ with licence, freelancer), start and planned end date.
  • Internal owner and the steering contact on the vendor side.
  • Risk class (low, medium, high, critical) with a one-line rationale.
  • Access rights granted and date of last access review.
  • Date of last status review and next review date.

2. Gates at entry, during and at exit

3. Clear roles

The system fails when everyone assumes someone else checks. A simple allocation works for companies with 50 to 500 employees: procurement owns the register and the entry gate, engineering owns the operational reality (how work is assigned), HR or legal owns the status assessment and the escalation, and management receives a quarterly summary with the number of engagements per risk class. Under §266a StGB and §823 Abs. 2 BGB, managing directors carry personal exposure for misclassification, so their visibility is not a formality.

4. Rules of engagement for engineering leads

Most status risk is created in daily work, not in contracts. A one-page guideline for engineering leads should cover:

  • Requirements go to the vendor's lead, who assigns tasks within the vendor team.
  • External engineers are not listed in internal org charts or HR tools.
  • Planning meetings with external teams are coordination, not task assignment to individuals.
  • Results are accepted formally at the agreed points, under §640 BGB where a Werkvertrag applies.
  • Any request to "just add the freelancer to the team" goes through the quarterly gate first.

Common failure patterns

Three patterns appear repeatedly in German companies. First, the register exists but only covers procurement-managed vendors, while freelancers paid through engineering budgets are missing. Second, contracts are reviewed once at signing and never again, even when the engagement runs for three years and the scope has changed completely. Third, the rules for engineering leads are written but never trained, so the operational reality drifts towards integration. Each of these is visible in a routine audit, and §25 SGB IV allows contribution claims for intentionally withheld amounts to reach back thirty years.

Getting started in 30 days

  1. Week 1: pull every payment to external parties for the last 24 months from accounting, across all cost centres, and every external account from your identity provider.
  2. Week 2: merge both lists into the register and assign an internal owner to each entry. Entries without an owner are the first finding.
  3. Week 3: classify each entry by risk class with a one-line rationale, and flag every high and critical case for legal review.
  4. Week 4: present the summary to management, agree the gate owners and schedule the first quarterly review.

The first pass is usually the hardest, because it reveals engagements that nobody remembered. After that, maintaining the register takes a few hours per quarter.

Where managed teams simplify the system

A control system is easier to run when the external capacity is organised in a way that matches the register. A vendor-led team with its own lead, a written scope and sprint acceptance generates one register entry, one steering contact and one acceptance log, instead of a dozen individuals with separate risk profiles. Our pod model describes such a structure.
