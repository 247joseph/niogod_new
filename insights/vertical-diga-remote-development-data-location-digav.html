DiGA Development With a Remote Team: §4 DiGAV and Where Data May Go

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

Digital health applications reimbursed by German statutory health insurance, the DiGA, operate under a data location rule that is stricter than the general DSGVO regime. Many DiGA manufacturers are small companies under heavy pressure to ship features, run studies and maintain several platforms, and they look for remote engineering capacity like any other software company. They can use it, but the setup must be designed around one fact: under § 4 Abs. 3 DiGAV, personal data processed by the DiGA may in principle only be processed in Germany, in another EU member state, in an equivalent state under § 35 Abs. 7 SGB I, or in a third country with an adequacy decision under Art. 45 DSGVO. India has no such adequacy decision. Standard contractual clauses, which carry most other EU to India arrangements, are not named in the provision.

The market behind the rule

The DiGA segment is small but established. According to the GKV-Spitzenverband's DiGA report covering September 2020 to the end of 2025, 58 DiGA were listed in the BfArM directory at 31 December 2025, 48 of them permanently and 10 provisionally, while 16 of the 74 applications ever listed had been removed again. Around 1,6 million activation codes were redeemed over the period, about 695.000 of them in 2025, and statutory insurers spent about 401 million EUR in total, 171 million EUR of it in 2025.

The same report shows the economic pressure on manufacturers: the average manufacturer price in the first year was 544 EUR, while the average negotiated reimbursement amount was 227 EUR. Margins after price negotiations are thin, which is exactly why manufacturers look for engineering capacity that costs less than a full local team, and why getting the data architecture right first matters so much.

What the rule means for engineering work

The safest working assumption is that any access by a remote engineer to production systems containing personal or health data of DiGA users constitutes processing for the purposes of § 4 DiGAV, including read access during support or debugging. German data protection authorities generally treat remote access as processing, and the data protection conference has published a resolution on cloud-based DiGA that underlines how strictly the location requirement is read. The consequence is a clean separation.

Engineering patterns that make this practical

Synthetic data as a product

A realistic synthetic data generator, covering diary entries, questionnaire results, device readings and edge cases, is the foundation. It should be versioned and maintained like production code. Remote engineers who can reproduce any user flow with synthetic data rarely need production access at all.

Logs without personal data

Structured logging with pseudonymous technical identifiers and strict field allowlists allows a remote engineer to diagnose most defects. A small EU-based on-call role can resolve the remaining cases and pass anonymised findings to the development team.

Infrastructure boundaries

Production runs in an EU region with access controlled by identity and location policies. Pipelines deploy from artefacts built in CI, so the remote team ships code without holding production credentials. Every exception is logged and reviewed.

Regulatory documentation

Most DiGA are also medical devices under the MDR, so the development process must satisfy IEC 62304 lifecycle requirements and the manufacturer's quality management system. A remote team fits into that system as a supplier with defined responsibilities, and the separation of data access becomes part of the documented supplier controls. The BSI technical guideline TR-03161 on security requirements for health applications provides further concrete expectations for the application itself.

Where setups typically break

The separation usually fails at the edges rather than in the core application. Crash reporting and product analytics tools are the first candidates: they are often added by a developer in an afternoon, run on servers outside the EU and capture identifiers or free text from users. Support ticket systems are the second: a user describes symptoms in a ticket, the ticket is forwarded to the development team, and health data has travelled without anyone noticing. Third, test data drifts: someone copies a small production extract into staging to reproduce a bug, and staging is accessible to the remote team. Each of these can be prevented with a written rule, an automated check or a tool configuration, but only if someone owns the topic. In practice the Projektleiter or product owner should review the list of tools and data flows at every sprint review, not only at the annual audit.

A checklist for DiGA manufacturers

  1. Map every system that holds personal data of DiGA users, including analytics, support tools and crash reporting.
  2. Confirm the location of each system and each subprocessor against § 4 Abs. 3 DiGAV.
  3. Define which roles may access these systems and ensure they are EU-based.
  4. Build and maintain a synthetic data generator before onboarding a remote team.
  5. Enforce log field allowlists and verify them in automated tests.
  6. Separate build and deploy credentials; the remote team never holds production secrets.
  7. Document the supplier relationship in the QMS, including data access boundaries.

The legal points above are general information and not legal or regulatory advice. The DiGAV has been amended several times, and the interpretation of remote access should be confirmed with your data protection officer and regulatory counsel before any arrangement is set up.
