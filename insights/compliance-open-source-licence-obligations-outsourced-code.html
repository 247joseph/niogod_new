Open Source Licence Compliance When an External Team Writes Your Code

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

Every engineering team uses open source, and an external team is no exception. What changes when the code is written by a vendor is who notices the licence terms. The obligations of the GPL, LGPL, AGPL, MPL or Apache licences attach to whoever distributes or, in some cases, makes the software available over a network. That is usually you, not the team that added the dependency. For DACH companies buying engineering capacity from India, open source compliance therefore belongs in the contract, in the definition of done and in the acceptance process under §640 BGB. This guide explains where the risk sits and how to build a control that works sprint by sprint.

The scale of the issue

The 2026 Open Source Security and Risk Analysis (OSSRA) report by Black Duck, based on audits of 947 commercial codebases, found open source in 98 percent of them. The average application contained 1.180 open source components. 68 percent of the audited codebases contained licence conflicts, and 87 percent contained at least one known vulnerability. These are codebases that were audited, often in the context of transactions, so they are not a random sample. They do show what an acquirer or investor is likely to find when it looks.

Internal use without distribution triggers few obligations for most licences. The moment firmware ships on a machine, an app is published or a SaaS product is built on an AGPL component, the analysis changes. For industrial and medtech companies, firmware is the most common surprise: a GPL component in an embedded Linux image is a distribution to every customer who buys the device.

Why the vendor relationship matters legally

Under German copyright law, a violation of licence conditions can lead to injunctive relief and damage claims against the party that distributes the software, under §§97 ff. UrhG. German courts have enforced GPL conditions since the mid 2000s. The external team is not the distributor in a typical Werkvertrag. It supplies a work result to you. If that work result contains a component whose licence you cannot comply with, the defect is in the delivery, and your remedy is contractual. That is why the contract has to say what the vendor may import and what it must disclose.

Contract clauses that do the work

  • Approved licence list. An annex that classifies licences as allowed, allowed with approval and prohibited for the specific product. AGPL and non-commercial licences usually need explicit approval.
  • Disclosure duty. The vendor delivers a software bill of materials (SBOM) with each increment, in SPDX or CycloneDX format, listing components, versions and licences.
  • Acceptance criterion. No Abnahme for an increment that introduces an unapproved licence or lacks an updated SBOM.
  • Warranty. The vendor warrants that the work result can be used and distributed as described in the Leistungsbeschreibung without violating third-party licences, subject to the approved list.
  • Remediation. The vendor replaces non-compliant components at its own cost within a defined period.
  • No copied code. Code from forums, AI assistants or other clients is subject to the same rules as any other third-party material.

A sprint-level control

  1. Scan on every merge. A software composition analysis tool in the CI pipeline flags new components and licence changes before they reach the main branch.
  2. Review at sprint end. The SBOM diff for the sprint is part of the acceptance package, alongside test results.
  3. Approve exceptions in writing. A short decision record for every component outside the allowed list: why it is needed, how the obligation will be met.
  4. Build the compliance artefacts. Attribution files, source offers for copyleft components and licence texts are generated per release, not reconstructed under pressure.
  5. Re-check before major events. A full scan before fundraising, an exit or a customer audit avoids surprises in the data room.

AI coding assistants

Code suggested by AI assistants raises the same question in a new form: a suggestion can reproduce licensed code without its notice. The practical answer is not a ban but a rule. Assistants with duplicate-detection filters switched on, the same SCA scan applied to all merged code regardless of origin, and a contractual statement that the vendor's use of assistants does not change its licence warranty.

The Cyber Resilience Act link

For products with digital elements, the Cyber Resilience Act requires manufacturers to identify and document components, including by drawing up an SBOM. The licence control and the vulnerability control therefore use the same inventory. Building one SBOM process now serves both, and it gives you an answer when an acquirer's technical due diligence asks for the dependency list.

This article is general information and does not constitute legal advice; licence interpretation for a specific product should be checked with counsel.
