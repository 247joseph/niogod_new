NIS2UmsuCG for Manufacturers: Turning BSIG Duties Into an Engineering Backlog

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

Germany's NIS2 implementation act has turned cybersecurity for many mid-sized manufacturers from a best practice into a statutory duty with personal management accountability. Most of the resulting work does not land in the legal department. It lands in engineering: asset inventories that include the shop floor, logging that supports a 24-hour report, vulnerability handling for in-house software, and security requirements for every supplier that touches production systems. The practical answer is to translate the statutory measures into a prioritised engineering backlog and deliver it like any other programme, with owners, acceptance criteria and evidence.

Where things stand

The NIS2 Implementation and Cybersecurity Strengthening Act (NIS2UmsuCG) entered into force on 6 December 2025 and substantially rewrote the BSI Act (BSIG). The federal government estimated that around 29,500 companies in Germany fall within scope. Entities had to register with the BSI by 6 March 2026; according to figures reported in summer 2026, only around 11,500 had done so by that date, and the BSI granted an extension to 31 July 2026. Fines for violations of risk management and reporting duties reach up to EUR 10 million or 2 percent of global turnover for particularly important entities, and up to EUR 7 million or 1.4 percent for important entities. Failure to register can itself be fined up to EUR 500,000.

The threat level justifies the attention. Bitkom's 2025 economic protection study put the damage to German companies from theft, espionage and sabotage at a record EUR 289.2 billion, with 87 percent of companies affected within twelve months and cyberattacks accounting for about 70 percent of the total.

Why manufacturers are in scope

NIS2 extends well beyond classic critical infrastructure. Manufacturing of machinery, motor vehicles, electrical equipment and electronic products is listed among the sectors whose medium and large companies can qualify as important entities. For a Mittelstand machine builder with more than 50 employees or more than EUR 10 million turnover, the first task is therefore a documented scope assessment, not an assumption that the law is meant for utilities.

From statute to backlog

Section 30 BSIG lists the risk management measures an entity must implement, Section 32 sets out the staged reporting duties (early warning within 24 hours, report within 72 hours, final report after one month), and Section 38 makes management responsible for approving and supervising the measures and for attending training. Translated into engineering work, the core items look like this:

Prioritisation for the first two quarters

  1. Scope and registration. Confirm whether and how the company is covered, register if not yet done, and document the assessment.
  2. Reporting readiness. The 24-hour early warning is the most time-critical duty. Build the minimal logging and on-call path that makes it achievable before anything else.
  3. Remote access. Vendor and service remote access to production is a frequent entry point. Inventory every path, enforce multi-factor authentication and log sessions.
  4. Asset inventory. Without it, neither vulnerability handling nor incident analysis works. Passive OT discovery tools speed this up but need validation on the floor.
  5. Supplier requirements. Update contract templates for integrators and software suppliers, then work through existing contracts by risk.
  6. Restore tests. Prove that a production line can be restored from backup, starting with the line whose outage costs most.

Measuring progress for management

Because management must approve and supervise the measures, progress should be reported in a form a managing director can read in five minutes. A small set of indicators works better than a long status document:

  • Share of production assets in the inventory with known owner and software version
  • Share of remote access paths with multi-factor authentication and session logging
  • Log coverage of the systems named in the incident response plan
  • Number of open critical vulnerabilities and their median age
  • Production lines with a successful restore test in the last twelve months
  • Suppliers with updated security clauses, weighted by risk

Reported quarterly, these figures show trend and turn a legal obligation into a manageable programme.

Common pitfalls

  • Treating OT as out of scope. Risk management covers the network and information systems the entity uses for its services, which in manufacturing includes production.
  • Policy without telemetry. An incident response policy that cannot be supported by log data will fail its first real test.
  • Management sign-off as formality. Section 38 BSIG ties management to the measures. Board-level reporting on backlog progress protects both the company and its directors.
  • One big-bang project. The work is continuous. Plan it as a backlog with quarterly priorities rather than a single project with an end date.

This article is general information and not legal advice; whether and how NIS2 applies to a specific company should be confirmed with qualified counsel.
