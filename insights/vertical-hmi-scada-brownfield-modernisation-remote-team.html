HMI and SCADA Modernisation: Running a Brownfield Migration Remotely

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

Many German plants run operator screens and supervisory software written fifteen or twenty years ago, on Windows versions that no longer receive security updates, maintained by one or two people who know where the scripts live. Replacing that software is unavoidable, but the plant cannot stop, and the internal automation team is fully occupied keeping production running. A remote engineering team can carry most of a modernisation, provided the work is sequenced so that the risky parts (discovery, cutover, commissioning) are owned on site and the large, repeatable parts (tag modelling, screen rebuild, test automation, historian migration) are delivered remotely against clear acceptance criteria.

Why now

Microsoft ended standard support for Windows 10 on 14 October 2025, and a large installed base of HMI stations and engineering workstations still runs on it or on older releases. At the same time, plants are being asked to deliver more data. Bitkom's Industrie 4.0 survey of 555 manufacturers, published in April 2026, found that 97 percent use at least one Industrie 4.0 application and 45 percent already use digital twins. A companion release from May 2026 named the brake: 48 percent see shortcomings in IT infrastructure as a reason such projects fail, and 57 percent say the necessary data is missing. Legacy SCADA is frequently where that data is locked.

Staffing is the second pressure. Bitkom's September 2026 labour market study found that IT administration and systems operations is the most sought profile, named by 26 percent of companies with vacancies, and that 20 percent of companies are already keeping older employees longer to cover gaps. For automation teams, that often means the person who wrote the original visualisation is close to retirement.

The sequence that keeps the plant running

  1. Inventory and freeze. Collect every project file, script, driver, licence and the PLC programme versions in use. Freeze changes to the old system except for safety-relevant fixes. On site, with remote support for cataloguing.
  2. Tag and alarm model. Extract tags, alarms and their limits into a structured, version-controlled model, ideally aligned with a standard information model such as OPC UA. This is the single largest piece of remote work and the foundation of everything else.
  3. Test bench. Build a simulation of the PLC side (soft PLC or recorded signal playback) so new screens and logic can be tested without touching the line. Remote build, on-site validation of signal behaviour.
  4. Screen and logic rebuild. Recreate screens from the model rather than copying pixel by pixel, applying a consistent style guide and alarm philosophy. Remote, in sprints, with operator reviews.
  5. Historian and reporting migration. Move historical data and reports, with reconciliation checks that totals and trends match. Remote.
  6. Parallel run and cutover. Run old and new side by side in read-only mode, compare values and alarms, then cut over line by line during planned stops. On site, with the remote team on call.

What to measure in each sprint

PhaseAcceptance evidence
Tag modelPercentage of legacy tags mapped, with exceptions listed and approved by the plant engineer
Test benchRecorded signal sequences reproduce known operating states
ScreensOperator walkthrough signed off; every alarm in the model visible and acknowledgeable
HistorianReconciliation report: aggregates over a defined period match within tolerance
Parallel runDeviation log closed; no unexplained value or alarm differences over an agreed number of shifts

These criteria translate directly into a sprint-based Leistungsbeschreibung with Abnahme per sprint, which keeps both the plant and the engineering team honest about progress.

Access without opening the plant network

The remote team does not need access to the production network for most of the work. Project files, the tag model and the test bench live in an engineering environment segregated from operations. Where live data is needed, a read-only replica or a one-way data diode feed is preferable to remote desktop access into the control layer. Any access to operational systems goes through a jump host with named accounts, session recording and time-limited approval by the plant. Companies covered by NIS2 transposition in Germany, or supplying operators who are, will want this documented as part of supplier security, and IEC 62443 provides a common vocabulary for zones and conduits.

This point on regulatory scope is general information and not legal advice; whether NIS2 duties apply to your organisation should be checked with your own advisers.

Typical traps

  • Starting with screens. Rebuilding screens before the tag model exists produces a second undocumented system.
  • Copying the alarm flood. Legacy systems often carry hundreds of nuisance alarms. Migration is the moment to rationalise them with operations, not to replicate them.
  • Ignoring licences. Runtime and driver licences for the new platform need to be ordered and tested early, especially for older PLC protocols.
  • Cutover without a rollback. Each line needs a tested way back to the old system for the first shifts after cutover.

Estimating duration and effort

An illustrative case for a plant with three lines and a few thousand tags: inventory and tag model usually form the largest single block, because every exception has to be clarified with the plant engineer. Screen rebuild and historian migration then run in parallel in manageable sprints. The cutover itself is tied to planned stops and therefore often sets the end date more than development does. Plan the stop windows first and derive the sprint plan from them, not the other way round. Teams that look for stop windows only at the end often lose half a year while the software is already waiting.

Who owns what

The plant keeps ownership of the inventory sign-off, operator acceptance, safety-relevant logic and every cutover decision. The remote team owns the tag model, the test bench, the rebuilt application and the migration tooling, all under version control and handed over with documentation that the plant can maintain. That split protects production and makes the external team's output auditable.

A brownfield modernisation is mostly disciplined, repeatable engineering wrapped around a few high-risk on-site moments. niogod™ delivers the repeatable part as a pod under a Werkvertrag with Abnahme per sprint, led by a German-speaking Projektleiter who aligns with your automation engineers and shift leads on every cutover window. If an unsupported HMI estate is on your risk register, that is a good moment to plan the sequence.
