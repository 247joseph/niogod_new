Nearshore Labour Costs Are Converging: Revisit Location Assumptions for 2027

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

Many DACH engineering organisations chose their nearshore locations in Poland, Czechia or Romania years ago, based on a cost gap that looked stable. It is not. Labour costs in Central and Eastern Europe have been rising several times faster than in Germany, and the gap that justified the original decision narrows a little more every year. That does not make nearshore engineering a bad choice. Time zone, travel distance and EU legal frameworks remain real advantages. But a location strategy for 2027 should be based on where costs will be in three years, not where they were when the contract was first signed.

What the 2025 data shows

The IMK institute of the Hans-Böckler-Stiftung reported in August 2026 that hourly labour costs in the German private sector reached EUR 45,70 in 2025, an increase of 3,4 percent, the fifth lowest rise among the 27 EU member states. Measured in euros, labour costs rose 10,0 percent in Poland, 9,6 percent in Czechia and 10,6 percent in Romania in the same year.

Eurostat data for 2025 places the EU average hourly labour cost at EUR 34,9, up 4,1 percent, with Romania at EUR 13,6 and Bulgaria at EUR 12,0. Bulgaria recorded the highest annual increase in the EU at 13,1 percent. Statistics Poland reported that average gross monthly pay rose 7,5 percent year on year in the third quarter of 2025, to PLN 8.771,70.

These are economy-wide figures, not engineering salaries, and software engineers in Central and Eastern European capitals already earn well above national averages. But the direction is consistent: the gap is closing at several percentage points per year.

Why convergence matters more than the current gap

A cost comparison that looks only at today's rates answers the wrong question. An engineering location is a multi-year commitment: teams take time to build, knowledge accumulates, and switching is expensive. What matters is the gap over the life of the arrangement.

An illustrative calculation shows the effect. Assume a nearshore engineer costs 55 percent of a comparable German engineer today. If nearshore costs rise 9 percent per year and German costs 3,5 percent per year, the ratio reaches about 58 percent after one year, about 61 percent after two years and about 64 percent after three years. After five years it exceeds 70 percent. The saving that justified the decision shrinks by roughly a third over the period, before any change in productivity or management effort is considered.

Neither column is better in general. The comparison shows that the trade-off is shifting: the cost argument for nearshore weakens over time, while its proximity advantages stay constant. For India, proximity is the weaker side and scale and cost are the stronger side.

A five-step location review

  1. Project costs forward. For each location, apply a realistic annual cost increase for the next three to five years. Use national labour cost data as a floor and add a premium for engineering roles, which typically rise faster.
  2. Separate work by proximity need. Work that requires frequent on-site presence, hardware access or tight daily collaboration benefits most from nearshore. Work that can be specified in sprints with clear acceptance criteria travels well to any location.
  3. Assess talent depth for your stack. A location that is affordable but cannot supply embedded, ML or SAP specialists at the volume you need is not a real option.
  4. Include management cost. Count the internal time spent on coordination, travel and onboarding per location. It is often the largest hidden difference.
  5. Decide on a portfolio. Many DACH companies will end up with more than one location: nearshore for proximity-critical work, India for scalable, well-specified engineering, and the core in-house.

Questions for an existing nearshore contract

If you already run engineering in Central or Eastern Europe, three questions help assess the position before the next renewal. How have your provider's rates moved over the last three years, and how does that compare with the national labour cost data? Which price adjustment mechanism does the contract contain, and is it tied to an index or left to negotiation? And how much of the work in that location genuinely depends on proximity, as opposed to having been placed there because it was the default option at the time? The answers often show that part of the portfolio would be equally well served elsewhere, while another part clearly belongs where it is.

What to avoid

Do not move work only because of a cost projection. Relocating an established team destroys knowledge, and the transition costs can exceed several years of savings. The review is most useful for new work streams, for expansions, and for contracts that come up for renewal. It is also a useful lens for negotiating with existing nearshore providers, whose own costs are rising and who will pass those increases on.

Where personal data is processed outside the EU, transfer safeguards such as standard contractual clauses become part of the location decision; this is general information, not legal or tax advice.
