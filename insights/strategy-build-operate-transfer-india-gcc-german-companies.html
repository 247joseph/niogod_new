Build-Operate-Transfer in India: A Staged Route to a German-Owned GCC

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

Global capability centres (GCCs) in India have moved from a large-enterprise phenomenon to a Mittelstand option. The question many German CTOs and CFOs now ask is not whether an Indian engineering base makes sense, but when it makes sense to own one. Founding a subsidiary on day one ties up capital, management attention and legal setup before the operating model is proven. A staged path, often called build-operate-transfer (BOT), separates the two: a partner builds and runs the capability first, and ownership transfers only when defined gates are met. For companies below roughly 50 to 100 dedicated engineers in India, the partner phase is frequently the entire answer.

The market context in 2026

The scale of the Indian GCC ecosystem is no longer in question. The Nasscom and Zinnov India GCC Landscape 2026 counts more than 2,100 GCCs employing about 2.36 million people, with revenue close to 100 billion USD, and projects a workforce of about 3.5 million by 2030. GCCs added around 200,000 people in FY26, compared with about 110,000 added by IT services firms.

German companies are part of this shift. A report by the Indo-German Chamber of Commerce and ISG, covered in October 2026, found that more than 80 German companies operate over 150 GCC units in India with more than 130,000 professionals, and that GCCs set up by German Mittelstand firms grew 108 percent over five years. The EU and India concluded their free trade agreement on 27 January 2026, with the EU opening 144 services subsectors including IT services, which adds political stability to long-term planning once the agreement enters into force.

Why owning too early is risky

Competition for talent inside the GCC ecosystem is intense. Aon's 2026 salary study reports GCC pay rising 9.3 percent in 2026 and a projected 9.4 percent in 2027, with overall attrition in India at 18 percent in 2026. A newly founded captive with no employer brand in Bangalore or Pune competes with thousands of established centres for the same engineers. In parallel, it must solve entity setup, payroll, local compliance, office space, IT security and leadership hiring. Each of these is solvable, but together they consume the first year of a German company's attention, usually before a single roadmap item is delivered.

The staged model

1. Pilot3 to 6 monthsPartnerProve delivery on one defined work package
2. Operate12 to 36 monthsPartnerScale pods, build processes, document architecture and quality gates
3. Transfer decision3 to 6 monthsPartner, then youDecide against gates, plan entity and employee transfer
4. OwnOngoingYour Indian entityRun the GCC as part of your organisation

The value of the model lies in the gates, not in the phases. Without explicit gates, BOT becomes a vague promise that nobody exercises.

Five gates before taking ownership

  1. Scale gate. Is the steady-state team large enough to carry a local leadership layer, HR and administration? Below a few dozen engineers, the overhead of an own entity is hard to justify.
  2. Stability gate. Has attrition in the partner-run team been at or below market levels for at least four quarters? Transferring an unstable team transfers the instability.
  3. Process gate. Are delivery processes, documentation, code review and security controls written down and independent of individual people?
  4. Strategic gate. Does the work include IP or capabilities you must hold inside your own group for valuation, certification or export control reasons?
  5. Economic gate. Does a full cost model of the captive, including entity overhead, leadership, facilities and compliance, beat the partner fee at the planned scale?

If two or more gates fail, extend the operate phase. That is not a failure of the model. It is the model working as intended.

Contract points to settle at the start

  • A transfer option with a defined notice period and a pre-agreed fee formula, rather than a fee negotiated under time pressure.
  • Clear rules for the transfer of employment, assets, licences and documentation.
  • Usage and ownership rights to all work results from day one, independent of whether the transfer is exercised.
  • Non-solicitation clauses that do not block the transfer itself.
  • Data protection arrangements under DSGVO Art. 28 and Art. 44 ff., including Standardvertragsklauseln for transfers to India, which must be reviewed again when the receiving entity changes.
  • Tax structuring, including transfer pricing and permanent establishment risk, reviewed with advisers in both countries before phase 3.

When to skip the transfer altogether

Many companies plan for ownership and then find that the operate phase already gives them what they needed: stable capacity, documented processes and German-facing project leadership, without the fixed cost of a subsidiary. For a Series A/B scaleup with 20 to 100 engineers in total, a dedicated Indian entity is rarely the most efficient structure. The option to transfer still has value, because it keeps the partner honest and gives the board a credible long-term path.

The decision should be revisited annually against the five gates. Market conditions, the company's own growth and the regulatory environment all move.
