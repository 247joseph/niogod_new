VSOP and Equity in the Engineering Budget: Pricing Dilution per Hire

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

In German startups, a senior engineering offer usually has two parts: a salary that appears in the budget, and a virtual or real equity grant that does not. Because the equity component costs no cash at grant, it is often treated as free. It is not. Every grant dilutes existing shareholders or consumes a pool that investors have priced into the round, and a virtual share plan creates a cash obligation at exit that will be taxed as employment income. This guide explains how to put a number on the equity component of an engineering hire, so that the comparison between building a team and contracting capacity includes all of the cost.

How widespread employee equity is

According to the Bitkom startup report published in September 2025, based on a survey of 152 tech startups, 40 percent of German startups already offer employee participation and a further 47 percent could imagine doing so. Virtual shares are the dominant instrument at 28 percent, ahead of stock options at 9 percent and real shares at 8 percent. Only 23 percent of the startups with a programme open it to all employees. For senior engineering hires in competitive fields, an equity component is close to standard.

The instruments and their cost logic

Virtual share plans (VSOP)

A VSOP grants a contractual claim to a cash payment that mirrors the value of a number of shares at an exit event. No shares are transferred, so the cap table does not change. The cost appears later: at exit, the payment is usually made from the proceeds and is treated as employment income for the employee. Depending on the employee's other income that month, the payment may also trigger social security contributions up to the monthly ceilings. Founders often underestimate that the VSOP pool is economically equivalent to dilution, because buyers and investors account for it in the exit waterfall.

Real shares and options under §19a EStG

The Zukunftsfinanzierungsgesetz, in force since 15 December 2023, widened the deferred taxation regime of §19a EStG. As summarised by Carta, it now applies to companies with fewer than 1,000 employees and either revenue up to 100 million EUR or a balance sheet total up to 86 million EUR, founded no more than 20 years before the grant. Taxation of the benefit can be deferred for up to 15 years or until an exit. The general tax-free allowance for employee share benefits under §3 Nr. 39 EStG rose from 1,440 EUR to 2,000 EUR per year from 1 January 2024. The reform made real equity more practical, but many startups continue to use VSOPs for simplicity.

Putting a number on it

The useful figure for budget comparisons is the expected annualised value of the grant, not its theoretical upside. The following is an illustrative calculation.

InputAssumption
Post-money valuation at grant40 million EUR
Grant to a senior engineer0.15 percent, virtual
Notional value at grant60,000 EUR
Vesting4 years, 1-year cliff
Annualised notional value15,000 EUR per year

On this basis, the equity component adds about 15,000 EUR per year to the economic cost of the role, on top of salary and employer on-costs. Some CFOs apply a probability discount for the chance that no exit occurs. Others argue that the grant should be valued at the price the latest investors paid, because that is the price at which the company sold the same percentage. Either way, the number should be visible in the decision, not set to zero.

The pool is a budget, not a formality

Investors typically require an employee pool to be created or refreshed in each financing round, and the pool is often counted in the pre-money valuation, which means existing shareholders bear the dilution. A team plan that adds ten senior engineers over the next eighteen months consumes a measurable share of that pool. If the pool runs out before the next round, the company either grants less, which weakens offers, or negotiates a refresh, which costs founders more dilution.

How candidates value it

The cost to the company and the value perceived by the candidate are rarely the same. Experienced engineers who have seen several startups often apply a steep discount to virtual shares, because payout depends on an exit, on the waterfall and on leaver rules they cannot influence. A grant that costs the company 15,000 EUR per year in expected dilution may be valued by the candidate at a fraction of that. If equity does not move the candidate's decision, it is an expensive way to close a salary gap, and a transparent conversation about salary may serve both sides better.

Five questions before granting

  1. What is the annualised value at the last round price? Put that figure next to the salary in the offer approval.
  2. How much of the pool does the hiring plan consume? Model grants for every planned hire through the next round, not only the current offer.
  3. What happens on departure? Good leaver and bad leaver rules, vesting after termination and the treatment of vested virtual shares have been the subject of labour court decisions, and plans drafted years ago may need review.
  4. Who funds the payout? In a VSOP, the company owes cash at exit. The plan terms and the shareholders' agreement should make clear how that is settled.
  5. Is equity the right currency for this role? Equity makes sense for people who will shape the company over years. For capacity tied to a defined project, a cash-based arrangement is often fairer to both sides.

Equity and contracted capacity

External delivery models carry no equity component. That is not automatically an advantage, because equity aligns core engineers with the company's long-term outcome in a way no contract can. The point is to compare like with like. When a permanent hire is compared with a contracted team, the permanent side should include salary, employer on-costs, acquisition cost and the annualised value of the equity grant. Only then does the comparison show what the company is actually paying for each option.
