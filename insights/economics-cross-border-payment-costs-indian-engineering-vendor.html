Paying an Indian Engineering Vendor: Bank Fees, FX Spreads, Timing

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

When a German company pays an engineering provider in India, the invoice amount is rarely the whole story. A cross-border transfer outside SEPA passes through correspondent banks, each of which may deduct a fee, and at some point the euros are converted into rupees at a rate that includes a spread. Who bears these costs depends on choices that are often made by default in the accounts payable system rather than by agreement. The amounts per transfer are small, but over a multi-year engagement they add up, and unclear allocation creates recurring disputes about short payments. This field note explains the mechanics, the current data and a checklist for finance teams.

Why the transfer is not like a domestic payment

SEPA covers euro payments within the SEPA area at domestic cost. India is outside it, so payments travel through SWIFT and one or more correspondent banks. The global picture is improving only slowly. The Financial Stability Board noted in its October 2025 progress report on the G20 roadmap for cross-border payments that key performance indicators showed only a slight improvement at the global level since they were first calculated in 2023, and that satisfactory improvement by the 2027 target date is unlikely. Among the G20 targets for retail cross-border payments by end of 2027 are an average cost of no more than 1 percent and no corridor above 3 percent. According to a summary of the FSB data by The Paypers, 18.3 percent of corridors still exceeded the 3 percent mark in 2025, and only 35.4 percent of payments reached the recipient within one hour.

The three cost layers

1. Sending bank fees

Your bank charges a fee for the international transfer. It is visible on your statement and usually modest.

2. Intermediary and receiving bank fees

Correspondent banks and the receiving bank in India may deduct their own fees from the amount in transit. Whether these are charged to you or to the recipient depends on the charge option on the payment order.

Charge optionMeaningEffect
OURSender pays all feesFull invoice amount should arrive; sender may receive later fee claims
SHAFees are sharedSender pays own bank, recipient bears intermediary and receiving fees; arrival amount is lower than invoiced
BENRecipient pays all feesAll fees deducted from the transfer

SHA is the default in many systems. It is legitimate, but if the contract states that the invoice amount must be received in full, every SHA payment creates a short payment that the vendor will invoice again or carry as an open item.

3. Currency conversion

If the invoice is in EUR, the conversion into INR happens in India and the vendor bears the spread. If the invoice is in INR, your bank converts and you bear it. Bank spreads vary widely by provider and amount and are rarely shown as a separate fee, which is why they are easy to miss.

The exchange rate itself

Spreads are a cost; exchange rate movements are a risk. In 2026 the euro traded against the rupee between a high of 112.63 on 20 May and a low of 101.14 on 23 November, according to historical data published by Pound Sterling Live, a range of roughly 10 percent within one year. For a vendor invoicing in EUR with costs in INR, a weaker euro means less rupee revenue for the same invoice. That is why many contracts address currency allocation explicitly, separately from the payment mechanics discussed here.

Timing rules on the Indian side

Indian exporters of services are subject to foreign exchange regulation administered by the Reserve Bank of India. By a notification of 5 June 2026, the RBI restored the period within which the full export value must be realised and repatriated to nine months from the date of export, after a temporary extension to fifteen months. In practice, this means your vendor has a regulatory interest in prompt and complete payment, and long payment terms or unresolved short payments create compliance work on their side. Indian banks also require a purpose code and supporting documentation for incoming remittances, so a clear payment reference matters.

An illustrative calculation

The following is an illustrative calculation, not a quote from any bank. It assumes a monthly invoice of 25,000 EUR.

ItemAssumptionAnnual effect
Sending bank fee25 EUR per transfer300 EUR
Intermediary and receiving fees30 EUR per transfer360 EUR
Conversion spread, bank standard1.0 percent3,000 EUR
Conversion spread, negotiated or specialist provider0.3 percent900 EUR

In this example, the conversion spread is the largest item by far, and choosing the conversion route matters more than the fixed transfer fees. Whoever bears the spread under the contract has an incentive to optimise it.

The German tax side

Invoices from a provider established outside Germany for services to a German business are typically subject to the reverse charge procedure under §13b UStG. The invoice shows no German VAT, and the recipient declares and, where entitled, deducts the VAT. Accounts payable should know this before the first invoice arrives. This note is general information, not tax advice, and the treatment of a specific contract should be confirmed with your tax adviser.

Checklist for finance and accounts payable

  • Agree the invoice currency and who bears conversion costs in the contract, not by system default.
  • Set the charge option deliberately. If the contract requires full receipt, use OUR or gross up.
  • Quote the invoice number and purchase order number in the payment reference.
  • Pay on a fixed date each month so that the vendor can plan its own conversion.
  • Compare the conversion spread of your house bank with at least one alternative once a year.
  • Reconcile short payments monthly rather than letting them accumulate.
  • Record the reverse charge treatment in the vendor master data.
