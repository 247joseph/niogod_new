Baby Boomer Retirements and Legacy IT: A Knowledge Succession Plan

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

In many Mittelstand companies, the most critical IT knowledge sits in the heads of a few long-tenured engineers: the ABAP developer who wrote the pricing logic in 2004, the automation engineer who knows why the PLC interface behaves as it does, the administrator who maintains the batch jobs nobody documented. Germany's retirement wave is now removing these people at scale, and replacing the headcount does not replace the knowledge. The answer is a deliberate knowledge succession plan that starts two to three years before the retirement date, combines documentation with structured shadowing, and assigns each legacy system a future owner, internal or external.

The size of the wave

The Institut der deutschen Wirtschaft (IW Köln) quantified the demographic pressure in June 2026. Of the roughly 20 million people in the 1954 to 1969 birth cohorts, about 15 million will reach retirement age by 2036, around 1.3 million per year. Only about 800,000 young people enter the labour market annually, leaving a net loss of roughly 500,000 potential workers per year and a cumulative gap of about 4.3 million by 2036, up from IW's earlier estimate of 3 million.

IT is not exempt. Even as the overall IT shortage eased to 79,000 open positions in Bitkom's 2026 study, 76 percent of surveyed companies expect it to worsen again. The DSAG, the German-speaking SAP user group, explicitly names skills shortages as one reason why many members will not finish their S/4HANA migration before ECC mainstream maintenance ends in 2027. Retirements turn this into a double problem: the people who understand the old system leave just as the migration away from it needs them most.

Why legacy knowledge loss is different from a vacancy

A normal vacancy costs output. A legacy knowledge loss costs understanding. Three characteristics make it dangerous:

  • It is invisible until it breaks. A batch job that runs every quarter end will not reveal that nobody understands it until it fails at quarter end.
  • It is undocumented by design. Systems built incrementally over twenty years encode business rules in code, not in specifications.
  • It cannot be hired back. The market does not supply people who know your customising. Every successor starts from zero.

KfW Research adds a structural point: only 30 percent of Mittelstand companies completed a digitalisation project in 2022 to 2024, with total spending falling to 23.8 billion EUR. Many firms therefore carry older system landscapes into the retirement wave, with less modernisation behind them than their larger peers.

A five-step succession plan

Step 1: inventory knowledge at risk

List every system that supports a revenue, production or compliance process. For each, record the people who can change it safely, their age band and expected exit date. Any system with one knowledge holder leaving within 36 months is a priority.

Step 2: score and rank

Score each system on three axes from 1 to 5: business criticality, documentation quality (inverted), and time to exit of the knowledge holder. Multiply the scores. The ranking tells you where to start, and it gives management a defensible basis for budget.

Factor1 (low risk)5 (high risk)
CriticalityInternal reporting onlyOrder intake, production control, regulated records
Documentation gapCurrent architecture and runbooks existCode is the only documentation
Time to exitMore than five yearsLess than twelve months

Step 3: extract knowledge in the right format

Interviews alone produce stories, not usable knowledge. Combine three formats: architecture and data flow diagrams drawn together with the knowledge holder, runbooks for recurring operations, and recorded walkthroughs of the most complex code paths. Automated code analysis can now generate first drafts of documentation from legacy code, which the expert then corrects. Correction is faster than authorship, and it uses the expert's remaining time efficiently.

Step 4: structured shadowing with a named successor

Assign a successor who performs real changes under supervision for at least six months before the exit date. The successor does not need to be an employee. A defined external team can take over maintenance of a legacy system, provided the handover is planned as a work package with acceptance criteria rather than an informal transfer.

Step 5: decide the long-term path for each system

Once knowledge is secured, each system has three options: maintain as is with a new owner, modernise incrementally, or replace. The succession plan buys you the time to make this decision on business grounds rather than under the pressure of an unexpected failure.

Common mistakes

  • Starting six months before retirement. Meaningful transfer for a complex system takes much longer.
  • Treating the retiring engineer as a documentation resource only, instead of involving them in decisions about the system's future.
  • Assuming the replacement hire will "figure it out". Without overlap, they will rebuild understanding through incidents.
  • Ignoring part-time retirement models. A consulting arrangement with a retiree can bridge critical phases, but it must be structured carefully under German social security rules.

Where to put the budget

Succession work competes with feature work and usually loses. Frame it for management in risk terms: the cost of a production stoppage or a failed quarter close against the cost of a twelve month transfer programme. The IW figures make the case that this is not a one-off event but a sustained trend through 2036, so the transfer capability you build for the first system will be reused for the next.

Legacy knowledge transfer works best when it is run as a defined engagement with clear deliverables, not as an informal side task. niogod™ structures such handovers through a German-speaking Projektleiter who works directly with the departing expert, while the Bangalore team documents, shadows and then takes over maintenance under agreed acceptance criteria. If a key retirement is due within the next three years, this is a conversation worth having early.
