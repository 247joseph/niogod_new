Security Clearance Under the SÜG: Staffing Defence Programmes With Mixed Teams

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

German defence and dual-use startups are growing faster than their ability to clear people. Every engineer who touches classified material on a contract with the Bundeswehr or a prime contractor needs a security check under the Sicherheitsüberprüfungsgesetz (SÜG), and those checks take months. Engineers outside Germany, including an offshore team, will in most cases not be cleared at all. The answer is not to avoid external capacity, but to structure the product and the programme so that a small cleared core holds the classified parts and the critical decisions, while a larger team builds the unclassified platform, tooling and civil variants. Done well, this also simplifies export control.

The scale of the pressure

Capital is flowing into the sector. Dealroom and the NATO Innovation Fund reported in February 2026 that European defence, security and resilience startups raised a record 8,7 billion USD in 2025, up 55 percent year on year, with German companies taking 2,1 billion USD, second only to the United Kingdom. Demand from the export side is also substantial: the Federal Ministry for Economic Affairs reported in February 2026 that export licences for military equipment in 2025 totalled around 12 billion EUR on preliminary figures.

The clearance system is the bottleneck. At a Bundestag committee hearing in November 2025 on the SÜG reform, the security industry association BDSW cited processing times of three to six months for standard security checks and five to eight months for sabotage protection checks. On 4 December 2025 the Bundestag passed a modernisation of the SÜG that makes internet research a standard measure and creates the legal basis for digitising procedures. Bitkom's 2026 position paper on practical classified information protection still describes processing times of several months and calls, among other things, for clearances tied to the person rather than the employer and for the ability to start checks for key staff before a contract is awarded.

VS-NUR FÜR DEN DIENSTGEBRAUCH does not require a personnel check, but it requires handling rules and an obligation of the company. Persons without a long residence history in Germany are, in practice, very difficult to check, which means offshore engineers should be planned as uncleared from the start.

Designing the programme around a cleared core

1. Classify the architecture, not just the documents

Ask early which components, interfaces, data sets and parameters will be classified once a contract is signed. Threat libraries, specific waveforms, target signatures, mission data and certain performance parameters are typical candidates. Middleware, user interface frameworks, simulation infrastructure, build systems and test tooling usually are not. Draw the boundary into the architecture so that classified modules plug into an unclassified platform through narrow, documented interfaces.

2. Size the cleared core realistically

The cleared team owns the classified modules, integration with customer systems, and the final test with real parameters. With months of lead time per check, start the process for key people as soon as the opportunity is concrete, and count on attrition: not every check succeeds, and cleared engineers are in demand elsewhere.

3. Let the wider team build everything else

An uncleared team, including a remote one, can build the platform with synthetic or open parameters, maintain CI pipelines, simulation environments and hardware abstraction layers, and develop civil variants of the product. This is often the majority of the code base by volume.

4. Keep export control aligned

Even unclassified software or technical data for military or dual-use products may require export authorisation before it is shared with a team abroad, under the EU Dual-Use Regulation 2021/821 or national rules for military goods. The same architectural boundary that separates classified from unclassified work usually also separates controlled from uncontrolled technology, which makes classification by the BAFA or an internal export officer easier to obtain and to maintain.

A staffing sequence for a new programme

  1. Identify classified and export-controlled elements with the customer and your security officer.
  2. Draw the architectural boundary and document the interfaces.
  3. Nominate the cleared core and start checks for key people immediately.
  4. Obtain export classification for the unclassified platform work before involving any foreign team.
  5. Start the wider team on platform, tooling and civil variants with synthetic data.
  6. Integrate classified modules in a separate, accredited environment in Germany.

Putting a number on the wait

An illustrative calculation shows the effect: if a programme has to wait five months for each new engineer's check before that person can work productively on the classified part, every hire shifts the critical path by that period. If only a core of three to five people is checked and the remaining work on unclassified modules starts immediately, those months are gained for most of the code. The calculation is illustrative, but it explains why the architectural boundary is a commercial decision and not only a security one.

What this means for contracts

For the external part of the team, the Leistungsbeschreibung should state explicitly that work is limited to unclassified and export-cleared scope, that any change in classification triggers a stop and review, and which environment the work takes place in. That protects the company and the supplier, and it gives the customer's security officer a clear picture of who sees what.

This briefing is general information and not legal advice; classification, clearance and export requirements depend on the specific contract and should be confirmed with your security officer, the responsible authorities and legal counsel.
