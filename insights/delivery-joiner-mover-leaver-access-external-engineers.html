Joiner, Mover, Leaver: Access Control for External Engineering Pods

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

Security certifications describe what a vendor promises. Access management decides what actually happens on your systems every day. When a remote engineering pod works on your code, cloud accounts and ticketing, each engineer is an identity with permissions, and those permissions change as people join, switch tasks or leave. Most incidents involving third parties are not sophisticated attacks but stale accounts, shared credentials and access that nobody remembered to remove. The answer is a joiner, mover, leaver (JML) process owned by the client, executed with the pod, and reviewed on a fixed schedule.

The threat picture

The figures are clear. Verizon's 2025 Data Breach Investigations Report, which analysed 12,195 confirmed breaches, found that third-party involvement roughly doubled to about 30 percent of breaches, and that credential abuse remained the most common initial access vector at 22 percent. IBM's Cost of a Data Breach Report 2025 put the average cost of a breach in Germany at EUR 3.87 million, and reported that 16 percent of German cases began through supply chain or third-party access, at an average of EUR 4.52 million per incident. Bitkom's 2025 study on economic security estimated total damage to German companies from theft, espionage and sabotage at about EUR 289.2 billion, with 87 percent of surveyed companies reporting they had been affected.

An external pod is, by definition, third-party access. That is not a reason to avoid one. It is a reason to manage identities with more discipline than many companies apply to their own staff.

Principles

  • Client-owned identities. Pod engineers receive accounts in the client's identity provider, not shared vendor accounts. Each account maps to one named person.
  • Least privilege by role. Access is granted per role tier, not per request.
  • Strong authentication. Phishing-resistant multi-factor authentication for all accounts, with no exceptions for service desks or "temporary" access.
  • Time limits. Elevated access, such as production read or administrative rights, is granted for a defined period and expires automatically.
  • Single source of truth. One access register lists every pod identity, its tier and its approver.

Access tiers for a three-person pod

TierTypical accessApproverDefault duration
1: CollaborationEmail, chat, ticketing, documentationClient product ownerEngagement term
2: DevelopmentSource repositories, CI pipelines, development and staging environmentsClient engineering leadEngagement term
3: Production readLogs, monitoring dashboards, read-only database replicas with masked dataClient CTO or security owner90 days, renewable
4: Production changeDeployment rights, infrastructure changesClient CTO and security ownerPer change window or incident

Most pod work needs tiers 1 and 2. Tier 3 should be justified by operational duties. Tier 4 should be the exception and logged per use.

Joiner: before the first day

  1. The pod's Projektleiter submits a request naming the person, role and required tier.
  2. The client approver confirms and the client's IT creates the account in its identity provider.
  3. The engineer enrols a hardware key or approved authenticator and confirms the device meets the agreed baseline: managed device, disk encryption, current patches, endpoint protection.
  4. The engineer acknowledges the client's acceptable use and data handling rules.
  5. The access register is updated with start date and next review date.

Mover: when work changes

Movers are the most neglected category. When an engineer shifts from one component to another, or from development to an operations rotation, permissions are typically added and never removed. Treat every change of responsibility as a new request: grant what the new role needs, revoke what the old role no longer requires, and record both in the register. A useful rule is that an access increase is only approved together with a stated reduction or a confirmation that none applies.

Leaver: same day, not same month

When a pod engineer leaves the engagement, whether through rotation, resignation or a scale-down, revoke access on the last working day. The checklist:

  • Disable the account in the identity provider, which removes single sign-on access to connected tools.
  • Remove direct grants that bypass single sign-on: cloud console users, database accounts, VPN profiles, repository deploy keys.
  • Rotate any secrets the engineer could have seen, in particular shared service credentials and API keys.
  • Revoke personal access tokens in source control and CI systems.
  • Transfer ownership of scheduled jobs, dashboards and documents to a named successor.
  • Confirm deletion of client data on the engineer's device, documented by the vendor.
  • Close the entry in the access register with the date and the person who verified it.

Secrets and service accounts

Human identities are only half of the picture. Pipelines, test harnesses and integrations run on service accounts and API keys, and these are where access tends to leak. Keep all secrets in the client's secrets manager, never in repositories, chat messages or local configuration files. Give each service account a named human owner on the client side and an expiry date. Enable secret scanning on every repository the pod works in, so that a key committed by mistake is detected and rotated within hours. When a pod engineer leaves, the list of secrets to rotate should come straight from the secrets manager's access log rather than from memory.

Quarterly recertification

Once a quarter, the client's security owner and the pod's Projektleiter review the access register line by line. Each identity is confirmed, reduced or removed. Accounts unused for 30 days are flagged. The review takes under an hour for a single pod and produces a dated record, which is useful evidence for ISO 27001 or TISAX audits and for the technical and organisational measures required under Art. 32 DSGVO.

Contractual anchors

The data processing agreement under Art. 28 DSGVO and the security annex of the delivery contract should reference the JML process explicitly: notification periods for leavers, the vendor's duty to report changes in staffing, device requirements and the right to audit. Because the engineers work in India, transfers of personal data also need a valid transfer mechanism, typically standard contractual clauses with a transfer impact assessment. The legal points here are general information, not legal advice.

Good access hygiene is unspectacular, and that is the point. In a niogod™ pod, the Projektleiter maintains the access register with your security owner, files every joiner, mover and leaver request in writing, and runs the quarterly recertification with you. If you are preparing to bring an external team onto your systems, agreeing this process before the first account is created is the simplest control you can put in place.
