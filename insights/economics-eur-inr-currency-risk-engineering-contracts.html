EUR or INR: Allocating Currency Risk in Offshore Engineering Contracts

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

When a German company buys engineering services from India, someone carries the exchange rate risk between the euro and the rupee. Contracts often leave this implicit, and the cost appears later as a budget variance nobody planned for or as a vendor asking to renegotiate. The answer for most DACH buyers is simple: invoice in EUR at a fixed fee, let the vendor manage the rupee side, and write down explicitly how and when the price may be reviewed. This article explains why, with the figures behind it.

How large the currency movement actually is

The euro to rupee rate is not a stable planning constant. The European Central Bank's reference rate for EUR/INR stood at 101.85 on 20 November 2026, compared with a high of 111.96 on 21 August 2026 in the same three-month window, according to the ECB's published series. The average over that period was 108.28. A swing of almost ten percent in a single quarter is large enough to move a project budget, and the direction can reverse just as quickly.

Underneath the exchange rate sits the vendor's cost base. Aon's annual salary survey projected average salary increases in India of 9.1 percent for 2026, and its 2026 study projects 9.1 percent again for 2027. Engineering talent in the leading hubs is in steady demand: Nasscom expects the Indian technology industry to reach revenues of USD 315 billion in fiscal year 2026. A vendor paying rupee salaries that rise at that pace while earning euros at a moving rate has a real margin problem, and that problem eventually reaches the client if the contract is silent.

Three invoicing models and who carries the risk

ModelWho carries FX riskBudget certainty for youTypical issue
Invoice in INRBuyerLow: euro cost changes every monthAccounts payable needs foreign currency payments; Controlling sees monthly variances
Invoice in EUR, fixed feeVendorHigh: fixed euro amount per periodVendor may price in a risk margin or ask for review after large moves
Invoice in EUR with FX bandSharedMedium to highRequires a clear reference rate, band and review mechanism

For a Series A or B scaleup, invoicing in INR is rarely worth it. The saving in a favourable month is small, the administrative load on finance is real, and the board sees an engineering line that moves for reasons unrelated to engineering. For a larger Mittelstand company with a treasury function that already hedges rupee exposure, INR invoicing can make sense, but it should then be part of the treasury's hedging programme, not a decision made in a statement of work.

Illustrative calculation: the same work at two rates

This is an illustrative calculation, not a quote. Assume a vendor invoices INR 2,000,000 per month. At the ECB reference rate of 111.96 (21 August 2026) the euro cost is about EUR 17,864. At 101.85 (20 November 2026) it is about EUR 19,637. The work is identical; the monthly cost difference is roughly EUR 1,770, or about ten percent. Over a twelve-month engagement, a movement of this size changes the annual cost by more than EUR 20,000 without any change in scope. If the same contract were invoiced in EUR at a fixed fee, the buyer's cost would stay flat and the vendor would absorb or hedge the movement.

The vendor's side: why rupee cash timing matters

Understanding the vendor's constraints helps when negotiating. Indian exporters of services must realise and repatriate export proceeds within a period set by the Reserve Bank of India under FEMA. According to Taxmann, the RBI reduced that period from 15 months back to 9 months with effect from 5 June 2026. Long payment terms or disputed invoices therefore create regulatory pressure for the vendor, not only cash flow pressure. A buyer who pays reliably on agreed terms in EUR is a buyer the vendor can price more keenly.

Clause patterns that work

  • Currency of account. "All fees are stated and invoiced in EUR. Exchange rate movements between EUR and any other currency are borne by the Contractor."
  • Review trigger, not automatic adjustment. "If the ECB EUR/INR reference rate, averaged over a calendar quarter, deviates by more than 10 percent from the rate at contract signature, either party may request a price review. Absent agreement, the existing fee continues to apply." This gives the vendor a voice without creating automatic price changes.
  • Annual review date. Fix one date per year when the fee may be discussed, linked to a documented cost basis. This prevents ad hoc requests in the middle of a release cycle.
  • Bank charges. State that each party bears its own bank charges (SWIFT option SHA) so that small deductions do not create open items in accounts payable.
  • Payment terms that match the vendor's obligations. Thirty days after invoice, with invoices issued after sprint acceptance, is a pattern both sides can plan around.

Note that automatic price indexation clauses in German contracts are subject to the Preisklauselgesetz and to AGB control, and that FEMA rules on the Indian side change from time to time. This article is general information and not legal or tax advice; please have clauses reviewed for your specific contract.

Questions to ask before you sign

  1. In which currency will invoices be issued, and is that stated in the contract?
  2. Does the price include a currency risk margin, and if so, how large?
  3. What exactly triggers a price review, and what happens if no agreement is reached?
  4. Which reference rate source and averaging period apply?
  5. Who bears bank and intermediary charges?
