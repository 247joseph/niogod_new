NIS2 in Germany: Supply Chain Duties When External Teams Write Your Code

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

Germany's NIS2 implementation law has been in force since 6 December 2025, and the registration deadline at the BSI passed on 31 July 2026. For the tens of thousands of companies now in scope, the next phase is less visible but more demanding: proving that cybersecurity risk management covers the supply chain, including the external teams that write, maintain or operate your software. For a CTO, the answer is to treat every external development team as a supplier under §30 BSIG, with defined security requirements, evidence and a joint incident process, rather than as extra capacity that inherits internal access rights.

The scope has grown sixfold

According to Reed Smith, the revised BSI Act expands the number of supervised organisations from about 4.500 to roughly 29.500 entities, which must classify themselves as "important" or "particularly important" entities. Many Mittelstand manufacturers, digital service providers and mid-sized software companies are among them for the first time.

Registration has lagged. Computerwoche reported in August 2026 that the BSI counted 18.845 registered entities when the deadline expired, against the roughly 29.500 expected, leaving a gap of more than 10.000. Fines under the BSI Act reach up to 10 million EUR or 2 percent of worldwide turnover for particularly important entities and up to 7 million EUR or 1,4 percent for important entities, following the ceilings of the NIS2 Directive.

The threat picture explains the pressure. Bitkom's Wirtschaftsschutz study, published in September 2025, put the damage to the German economy from theft, espionage and sabotage at 289,2 billion EUR, of which 70 percent, or 202,4 billion EUR, was attributed to cyberattacks.

What the law requires of the supply chain

§30 BSIG lists the risk management measures that in-scope entities must take, mirroring Article 21 of the NIS2 Directive. Supply chain security, including security aspects of the relationships with direct suppliers and service providers, is one of them, alongside secure development and maintenance, vulnerability handling, access control and incident handling. Management must approve these measures, monitor their implementation and attend training, and the BSI Act attaches personal accountability to that duty.

An external engineering team is a direct service provider in this sense. Its code goes into your product, its engineers may have access to repositories, build pipelines and sometimes production, and its own security posture becomes part of yours. In an eco survey of member companies reported by Computerwoche, 21 percent described the analysis and protection of their systems and supply chains as particularly difficult.

Reporting deadlines need a joint process

Particularly important and important entities must report significant incidents to the BSI in stages: an early warning within 24 hours, an incident notification within 72 hours and a final report within one month. If an incident starts in a supplier's environment, for instance through a compromised developer laptop or a poisoned dependency, your 24-hour clock may depend on how quickly the supplier tells you. The contract should therefore define:

  • A notification deadline for the supplier that is shorter than your own, for example a few hours after detection.
  • Named contacts on both sides, reachable outside business hours, with a tested escalation path.
  • The information the supplier must provide: affected systems, timeline, indicators of compromise, measures taken.
  • Cooperation duties for forensics, including log retention.

For teams in other time zones, this is a design question rather than a disadvantage. A team in India that works while Germany sleeps can extend monitoring hours, provided the escalation path is clear.

Contract structure matters for security, too

There is a link between NIS2 and labour law that is easy to miss. If external engineers are integrated into your organisation like employees, with internal accounts, internal tools and direct instructions from your leads, you inherit their security risk without the supplier controls that §30 BSIG expects, and you add AÜG and Scheinselbständigkeit risk. A vendor-led team under a Werkvertrag or Dienstvertrag can be managed as a supplier: requirements in the contract, evidence at acceptance, access through defined interfaces. Our security baseline lists how this is set up.

Individual freelancers with broad access rights are a particular concern. They rarely have their own security organisation, a certified management system or a substitute plan for emergencies. If such a person is unavailable or their laptop is compromised, the counterpart that §30 BSIG assumes is missing. With a vendor that has its own team, responsibilities, substitutes and evidence can be fixed in the contract and checked at every acceptance, which is a meaningful difference for the management that has to approve and monitor the measures.

A 90-day plan

  1. Weeks 1 to 2: inventory every external party with code or system access, including individual freelancers.
  2. Weeks 3 to 6: classify them by criticality and send the requirement table above to the critical ones.
  3. Weeks 7 to 10: amend contracts with incident clauses and evidence obligations.
  4. Weeks 11 to 13: run one tabletop exercise with your most critical supplier and record the outcome for management.

This article provides general information on the German NIS2 implementation and is not legal advice; whether and how your company is in scope should be assessed with qualified advisers.
