Definition of Ready for a Remote Pod: Keeping the Backlog Buildable

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

When a sprint with an external engineering team goes wrong, the post-mortem usually points to execution. More often, the cause sits earlier: a story entered the sprint with an unclear goal, a missing interface specification or an unanswered business question. With a pod working four time zones away, every such gap costs at least a day. The answer is a written Definition of Ready, agreed between client and pod, and a refinement routine that applies it before sprint planning rather than during the sprint.

Why readiness matters more at a distance

Unclear requirements are an old problem. PMI's Pulse of the Profession research on requirements management found that 47 percent of unsuccessful projects missed their goals because of inaccurate requirements management. PMI's 2020 Pulse report estimated that 11.4 percent of investment is wasted due to poor project performance. In a co-located team, a vague story is repaired over a desk in ten minutes. In a distributed team, the question waits for the next overlap window.

Developers feel this directly. In the Stack Overflow Developer Survey 2024, 53 percent said waiting for answers disrupts their workflow. Atlassian's State of Developer Experience Report 2025 found that 63 percent of developers believe their leaders do not understand their pain points, up from 44 percent a year earlier. A Definition of Ready is one of the few tools that addresses both: it reduces waiting and makes the pod's needs visible to the client.

A Definition of Ready for external delivery

The Scrum Guide does not prescribe a Definition of Ready, and it should not become a bureaucratic gate. For external delivery, however, a short checklist protects both sides. We recommend the following criteria for a story to enter sprint planning:

  1. Goal stated in business terms. One or two sentences explaining who benefits and why.
  2. Acceptance criteria written and testable. Each criterion can be checked by a person other than the developer. Where the engagement uses sprint-based acceptance, these criteria are the basis for it.
  3. Dependencies identified. External APIs, other teams, hardware, test data or licences are named, with an owner for each.
  4. Interfaces specified. Request and response formats, error cases and versioning are documented or linked.
  5. Open questions answered or explicitly parked. A parked question has a default assumption the pod may proceed with.
  6. Estimated by the pod. The team that builds it has sized it. A story too large for half a sprint is split.
  7. Non-functional constraints noted. Performance, security, data protection or regulatory requirements that apply.
  8. Test data and environments available. Or a task to create them is already in the backlog.

Who does what

RoleResponsibility in refinement
Client product ownerWrites the goal and first draft of acceptance criteria, answers business questions, sets priority
Client domain expert or architectClarifies interfaces, constraints and dependencies on the client's landscape
Pod ProjektleiterRuns the refinement session, tracks open questions, checks each story against the Definition of Ready
Pod Senior ConsultantChallenges technical feasibility, identifies risks, proposes splits
Pod Pilot EngineerEstimates, identifies missing test data and environment needs

The division matters. The client decides what is needed and why. The pod decides how to build it and whether the information is sufficient to do so. That boundary keeps the relationship clean and makes it obvious when a stalled story is waiting on the client rather than the vendor.

A refinement cadence that fits two time zones

  • Continuous drafting. The product owner writes and updates stories in the backlog throughout the sprint, not in a batch the day before planning.
  • Asynchronous pre-read. Two working days before refinement, the pod comments on each candidate story in writing: questions, proposed splits, missing items.
  • Refinement session, 60 to 90 minutes, mid-sprint. Held in the overlap window. Only stories with open comments are discussed.
  • Ready buffer. Aim to keep one and a half to two sprints of ready stories in the backlog. This absorbs absences, holidays and changes of priority.

Measuring readiness without adding overhead

Three simple numbers, reported at each sprint review, are enough to show whether the practice is working. The first is the ready buffer, expressed in sprints of estimated work that meet the Definition of Ready. The second is blocked time: the total hours stories spent waiting on a client answer during the sprint, taken from the ticket history rather than estimated. The third is churn in acceptance criteria: the number of stories whose criteria changed after sprint planning. None of these requires new tooling. Together they show, sprint by sprint, whether delays come from the backlog or from execution, and they give the product owner an objective basis for asking the business for faster decisions. Trends matter more than single values. A buffer that shrinks over three sprints is a warning, even if each individual sprint still looked fine.

Warning signs

  • More than one story per sprint is blocked for longer than a day by a business question.
  • Acceptance criteria are rewritten during the sprint.
  • The ready buffer falls below one sprint.
  • Estimates diverge widely between the first and second refinement of the same story.
  • The pod starts work on stories marked ready but quietly makes product decisions itself.

The last sign is the most important. A pod that fills gaps on its own may appear productive, but it shifts product decisions to the vendor without a record. Over time this weakens the client's control of its product and makes acceptance contentious. The better response is to stop, ask and park the story.

What to do with stories that are not ready

Do not pull them into the sprint "to keep the team busy". Instead, use slack capacity for agreed technical work: test coverage, documentation, dependency updates or debt reduction from a pre-approved list. That keeps the sprint honest and gives the product owner a clear signal that readiness, not capacity, is the constraint.

A Definition of Ready is a short document that changes behaviour on both sides of the contract. In a niogod™ pod, the German-speaking Projektleiter runs refinement against the agreed criteria and reports the ready buffer at every sprint review, so that the state of the backlog is visible before it becomes a delivery problem.
