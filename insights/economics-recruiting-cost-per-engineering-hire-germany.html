Recruiting Cost per Engineering Hire in Germany: Fees, Time, Ramp-Up

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

Most engineering budgets in Germany treat a new hire as a salary line that starts on the first working day. That framing hides a separate, front-loaded cost: the money and time spent acquiring the person in the first place. For a senior engineer, agency fees, internal interview hours and the ramp-up period before full productivity regularly add up to a sum in the range of a third to a half of one annual salary. This analysis breaks that acquisition cost into its components, shows how to calculate it for your own pipeline, and explains why the 2026 cooling of the IT labour market has not made it disappear.

The market context in 2026

The headline numbers suggest relief. In its study on the IT labour market published in September 2026, Bitkom counted 79,000 unfilled IT positions in Germany, down from 109,000 in 2025 and 149,000 in 2023. The Bundesagentur für Arbeit, in its bottleneck analysis released in September 2026, no longer classified software development as a shortage occupation for 2025. On paper, hiring an engineer should be easier and cheaper than at any point since the pandemic.

The same Bitkom survey, however, shows why acquisition cost remains high for the roles scaleups actually need. 97 percent of companies with open IT positions reported difficulty filling them, 31 percent said they receive practically no applications, and 33 percent said applicants lack knowledge of current technologies. The market has loosened at the generalist end and stayed tight for senior engineers with specific domain depth in AI, embedded systems, SAP or security. Those are exactly the hires where agency involvement and long processes are the norm.

The four components of acquisition cost

1. External fees

Contingency recruiters in the DACH region typically charge a percentage of the first-year gross salary. A 2026 fee overview published by the recruiting software provider Leonar puts typical fees for IT and engineering roles at 25 to 33 percent of annual salary, and cites the Bundesverband Deutscher Unternehmensberater with an industry average of 27.8 percent for success-based fees. Retained search for leadership roles sits higher and is paid in instalments regardless of outcome. Job board spend, sourcing tools and employer branding campaigns add a smaller but real amount per hire.

2. Internal time

Every interview loop consumes engineering hours. A typical senior hiring process involves a screening call, a technical interview, a system design or pairing session and a final conversation with the CTO or founder. Multiply that by the number of candidates who enter the loop for each hire made, and add the hours spent writing the job description, reviewing CVs and debriefing. These hours are rarely booked anywhere, but they are taken directly from roadmap capacity.

3. Time-to-hire

The weeks between opening a requisition and a signed contract are followed by the notice period of the candidate. In Germany, employed engineers frequently have contractual notice periods of one to three months, so a role opened in January often produces a first working day in May or June. The roadmap pays for that delay even if the budget does not.

4. Ramp-up

A new engineer does not deliver at full capacity in the first weeks. Access, codebase orientation, domain knowledge and team norms take time to absorb, and the colleagues who onboard the newcomer lose part of their own output during that period.

A worked calculation

The following is an illustrative calculation, not market data. Adjust each input to your own figures. For context, the Stepstone salary report 2026 puts the median gross annual salary of established software developers in Germany at 65,250 EUR, and of IT professionals overall at 66,750 EUR. Senior specialists sit well above those medians.

In this example the acquisition cost equals roughly 56 percent of the annual salary, and none of it buys delivered features. The calculation also excludes the cost of the vacancy itself, which is a separate question about lost output while the seat is empty.

Where the calculation breaks

Two risks multiply the result. The first is a failed hire. If the engineer leaves or is let go within the first year, most of the acquisition cost is sunk and the process restarts. Many agencies offer a replacement guarantee, but usually only for the first three to six months and only if the departure is not caused by the employer. The second is a stalled search. Bitkom found that 47 percent of companies cite missing German language skills as a hiring barrier, which narrows the pool further for teams that require German in daily work.

How to use the figure

  • Book acquisition cost as its own line. Show it next to salary in headcount plans so that the CFO compares like with like when external options are evaluated.
  • Amortise it over expected tenure. An acquisition cost of 47,540 EUR spread over a three-year tenure adds about 15,800 EUR per year to the cost of the seat.
  • Track your own conversion data. Candidates per hire, interview hours per hire and offer acceptance rate are the inputs that move the total most.
  • Separate scarce from available roles. For generalist roles the 2026 market may justify direct hiring without an agency. For scarce senior specialists, assume the full cost.
  • Compare against capacity that arrives without acquisition cost. External delivery models carry their own costs, but recruiting, notice periods and ramp-up risk sit with the provider rather than with you.

The decision this informs

Acquisition cost does not argue against hiring. Core engineers who will own your architecture for years justify the investment. It does argue for honesty in the comparison. A permanent hire that looks 20 percent cheaper than an external alternative on salary alone may be more expensive once the first-year acquisition cost, the delay and the failure risk are included.
