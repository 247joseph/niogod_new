Relocate or Remote: Hiring Indian Engineers to Germany vs Working in India

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

Many DACH CTOs now face the same fork when a senior role stays open: bring an engineer from India to Germany, or work with engineers who stay in India. Both routes draw on the same talent pool, and both are well established. They solve different problems, however. Relocation buys a person who sits in your office, joins your culture and becomes part of the permanent core. Remote delivery buys capacity and speed for defined scope, without the relocation lead time and without adding to German payroll. The right answer depends on the role, not on a general preference, and most growing engineering organisations end up using both deliberately.

The data behind both routes

Indian engineers are already a significant part of the German technology workforce. The Bundesagentur für Arbeit reported in July 2026 that Germany employed 1.15 million ICT specialists subject to social insurance in 2025, of whom 171,000 were foreign nationals, a new high and more than three times the 2015 level. Indians formed the largest group from outside the EU, with about 31,000 ICT specialists. IW Köln, using Bundesagentur data, found that Indians in academic STEM occupations in Germany rose to more than 32,800 in 2024, almost nine times the 2012 level, and that full-time Indian employees had the highest median wage of any nationality, at 5,393 EUR gross per month.

The legal route is established. The EU Blue Card requires a minimum gross annual salary of 50,700 EUR in 2026, or 45,934.20 EUR for shortage occupations, which include IT roles. For many senior engineering roles, market salaries in Germany are well above these thresholds.

The demand side is softer than in previous years but still selective. Bitkom's 2026 study counts 79,000 open IT positions, and 47 percent of companies with vacancies name missing German language skills as a hurdle. That last figure matters for the relocation decision, because a relocated engineer who does not speak German may face the same barrier inside a German-speaking organisation.

A role-based decision rule

Use four questions for each open role.

  1. Is physical presence needed regularly? Work on hardware, test benches, robots on a shop floor or customer sites favours relocation.
  2. Is this a long-term core role? Architecture ownership, technical leadership and roles that carry product knowledge for years favour relocation or local hiring.
  3. Can the work be specified as outcomes? Feature streams, integrations, test automation, data pipelines and platform migrations with clear acceptance criteria suit a team in India.
  4. How urgent is it? If a roadmap milestone depends on the role within the next two quarters, relocation lead times often decide the question.

A role with "yes" to the first two questions is a relocation or local hiring candidate. A role with "yes" to the last two is a remote delivery candidate. Mixed answers usually point to a combination: a relocated or local lead who works with a remote team.

Common mistakes on both sides

  • Relocating for capacity. Moving engineers to Germany to fill execution capacity carries German cost and months of lead time for work that could be delivered remotely.
  • Underestimating onboarding. Relocated engineers and their families need support with housing, registration and language. Without it, early departures are likely.
  • Treating remote teams as remote employees. Directing individual engineers in India through your own ticket system and stand-ups blurs the contractual model. Work through a provider's project lead and defined deliverables instead.
  • Ignoring language at the interface. Whichever route you choose, someone must translate between German-speaking business stakeholders and English-speaking engineers. Plan that role explicitly.

A simple cost comparison method

To compare the routes fairly, calculate the cost per productive month over the first two years rather than the monthly cost alone. For relocation, include search fees, visa and relocation support, the months before arrival in which the role stays open, onboarding time and a realistic probability of early departure. For remote delivery, include the provider fee, travel for workshops and acceptance meetings, and the internal time your team spends specifying and reviewing work. This framing often shows that relocation is the better investment for long-term core roles and remote delivery for defined scope with a deadline.

The combined model

In practice, growing DACH scaleups often converge on the same pattern: a permanent core in Germany that includes relocated international engineers in leadership and on-site roles, and one or more remote teams in India delivering defined scope. The relocated engineers frequently become the best bridge to the remote teams, because they understand both working cultures. Planning both routes together, rather than as competing budget lines, makes each more effective.
