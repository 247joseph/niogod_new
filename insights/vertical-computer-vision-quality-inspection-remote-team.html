Computer Vision for Quality Inspection: Splitting Work With a Remote Team

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

Automated visual inspection is one of the most requested AI use cases in German manufacturing, and one of the most frequently abandoned after the pilot. The reason is rarely the model. Projects stall because the image data does not reflect real production variance, because the lighting on the line differs from the lab, and because nobody owns the interface between the inspection result and the PLC that rejects the part. These problems are solvable, and the engineering behind them divides cleanly between what must happen at the plant and what a remote machine learning team can deliver.

The industrial context in numbers

Bitkom's Industrie 4.0 survey of 555 manufacturing companies, published in April 2026, found that 40 percent already use AI in production and a further 38 percent plan to. In a companion release from May 2026, the same survey showed the obstacle: 57 percent of manufacturers say the data they need is missing, and 48 percent see shortcomings in IT infrastructure as a reason digital twin projects fail. Inspection projects run into exactly these walls.

Automation keeps rising at the same time. The International Federation of Robotics reported in September 2026 that Germany installed 24.842 industrial robots in 2025, 41 percent of all installations in the EU. More automated cells mean more stations where a camera could replace a manual check, and more demand for engineers who can build such systems. Bitkom's September 2026 labour market study notes that 12 percent of companies with open IT roles are searching specifically for AI profiles.

Data is the contract, not the model

The most useful document in a vision project is a defect catalogue agreed with quality engineering: each defect class with a written definition, reference images, the acceptable limit and the consequence (scrap, rework, release). A remote team can build an excellent model against a good catalogue. Against an unclear one, it will optimise the wrong boundary, and the plant will reject the result.

A second rule: capture images from the production line from the start, not from a lab bench. Variance from shift changes, material batches, coolant residue and ageing light sources is what the model must learn. A practical step is to install the camera in shadow mode for several weeks, collecting images and the manual inspector's decision for each part, before any model is trained.

Acceptance criteria that work in a Leistungsbeschreibung

  • Escape rate (defective parts classified as good) below an agreed limit on a held-out test set from a defined production period.
  • False reject rate below an agreed limit, because every false reject costs rework time.
  • Inference time per part within the cycle time budget on the target edge hardware.
  • Model and dataset versioned together, with reproducible training from a tagged commit.
  • A documented procedure for adding a new defect class or product variant.

Images, people and works councils

Cameras on a production line can capture employees, which turns an inspection project into a processing of personal data under the DSGVO and potentially into a matter for the Betriebsrat, since § 87 Abs. 1 Nr. 6 BetrVG gives co-determination rights over technical systems suitable for monitoring behaviour or performance. Three engineering choices reduce the issue considerably: cropping or masking regions outside the part at the camera, transferring only part images to the remote environment, and documenting this in the data protection concept. If images with persons must leave the EU at all, the transfer rules of Art. 44 ff. DSGVO apply, and the simpler answer is usually to design them out.

A staged plan

  1. Weeks 1 to 3: defect catalogue workshop, camera and lighting trials on site, shadow mode capture begins.
  2. Weeks 4 to 8: remote team builds labelling tooling, dataset pipeline and baseline models; quality engineers label in short daily batches.
  3. Weeks 9 to 12: edge optimisation, PLC interface specification, operator screen; offline evaluation against the acceptance set.
  4. Weeks 13 to 16: on-site commissioning in advisory mode (the system flags, the human decides), then controlled switch to automatic reject for the defect classes that met their limits.

The schedule is an illustrative example; lighting problems or a missing defect catalogue can add weeks. The point is the order: data and definitions first, models second, line integration last.

What to keep in-house

Keep ownership of the defect catalogue, the final accept or reject decision on model releases, and the commissioning sign-off. These are quality and safety decisions. Everything between image capture and a validated model package is engineering work that can be delivered remotely and accepted against measurable criteria.

The notes on data protection and co-determination are general information and not legal advice; involve your data protection officer and, where relevant, the works council early.
