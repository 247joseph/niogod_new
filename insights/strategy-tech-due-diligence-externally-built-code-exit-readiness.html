Tech Due Diligence at Exit: How Acquirers Judge Externally Built Code

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

When a DACH scaleup approaches an acquisition, a late-stage round or a strategic investment, technical due diligence will ask a simple question: can the company prove that it owns and controls its software? If parts of the codebase were built by freelancers, agencies or offshore teams, this question gets sharper. External engineering itself is rarely a problem for an acquirer. Gaps in evidence are: unclear usage rights, undocumented open source, no record of who changed what, and knowledge concentrated in people who are not employees. The fix is an evidence pack built continuously, not reconstructed under deadline in a data room.

Why this matters more in 2026

Capital has returned to the German market. KfW Research counts about 9.5 billion EUR of venture capital invested in German start-ups by the third quarter of 2026, more than in any full year from 2023 to 2025. Larger rounds and renewed exit activity mean more companies will face institutional due diligence in the next two years.

At the same time, codebases have become harder to audit. Black Duck's 2026 Open Source Security and Risk Analysis, based on audits of 947 commercial codebases across 17 industries, found open source in 98 percent of them. License conflicts appeared in 68 percent of codebases, up from 56 percent a year earlier. The mean number of vulnerabilities per codebase rose 107 percent year over year, and the number of open source components grew 30 percent, which the report links to AI-accelerated code creation. Every additional component, and every line generated with AI assistance, is something an acquirer may ask you to account for.

Typical findings with external engineering

  • Freelancer contracts without clear rights grants. Under German law, the scope of usage rights in software follows the contract and its purpose. A vague contract can leave room for argument about exclusivity or scope.
  • Shared accounts and anonymous commits. If an external team pushed code through a single shared account, authorship and review cannot be reconstructed.
  • Undocumented open source brought in by a vendor. Components added without a licence check, sometimes under strong copyleft licences, appear only in the scan run by the acquirer's advisers.
  • Knowledge locked in a vendor. Critical subsystems understood only by an external team without documentation or a transition plan, which lowers the buyer's view of continuity.
  • Long-running individual contractors integrated into the team. Patterns that resemble employment raise questions about Scheinselbständigkeit or Arbeitnehmerüberlassung, which buyers price as contingent liabilities.

Building the evidence pack continuously

  1. Contracts: maintain a register of every external contributor with the contract, the rights grant and the period of work. Close gaps with confirmatory agreements while relationships are still good.
  2. SBOM and licence policy: generate an SBOM automatically in the build pipeline. Define allowed, restricted and prohibited licences, and log approved exceptions.
  3. Identifiable provenance: require individual accounts for all contributors, protected branches and mandatory review. Keep access logs.
  4. AI-assisted code policy: document which AI coding tools are permitted, under which settings, and how output is reviewed. Acquirers increasingly ask.
  5. Documentation and transition: keep architecture decisions, runbooks and a written transition plan for every external engagement.
  6. Contract structure for external teams: prefer structures where the provider directs its own team against defined deliverables, with acceptance records, over open-ended individual engagements.

Questions to rehearse before the data room opens

  • Which share of the current codebase was written by non-employees, and under which contracts?
  • Which copyleft components are in the product, and how are their obligations met?
  • How long do critical vulnerabilities stay open on average?
  • What happens to delivery if your largest external provider stops tomorrow?
  • How do you control code generated with AI assistance?

If the CTO can answer each question in two sentences with a document behind it, technical due diligence becomes a confirmation exercise rather than an investigation.

Timing

Start at least 12 to 18 months before an expected transaction. Fixing rights gaps, replacing problematic components and documenting subsystems takes engineering time that is hard to find in the middle of a sale process. A company that can hand over an SBOM, a contributor register and acceptance records on day one of due diligence shortens the process and removes arguments for a price reduction or a special indemnity.

This article provides general information and does not constitute legal advice; questions on usage rights, open source licensing and contractor status should be reviewed by counsel for your transaction.
