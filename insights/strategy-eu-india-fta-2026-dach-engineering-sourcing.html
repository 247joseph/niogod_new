The EU-India Free Trade Agreement: What It Changes for DACH Engineering

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

On 27 January 2026, the EU and India announced the conclusion of their free trade agreement at the 16th India-EU Summit, after negotiations that had stalled for most of the previous two decades. Most coverage focused on tariffs for goods. For DACH technology companies, the more relevant parts concern services and the movement of professionals. The agreement will not change how an engineering engagement with an Indian team works next month. It does reduce the political and regulatory uncertainty around a multi-year India strategy, and it gives CTOs and CFOs a stronger argument when boards ask whether building on Indian engineering capacity is a durable choice.

What was agreed on services and mobility

According to the Indian Ministry of Commerce announcement of 27 January 2026:

  • The EU opens 144 services subsectors to Indian providers, including IT and IT-enabled services, professional services and other business services. India opens 102 subsectors to EU providers.
  • Both sides commit to business mobility, including intra-corporate transferees and business visitors, with entry and work rights for dependants.
  • The EU offered commitments in 37 sectors for contractual service suppliers and 17 sectors for independent professionals.
  • India secured a five-year framework for talks on social security agreements with member states.
  • On goods, tariffs on about 33 billion USD of Indian labour-intensive exports fall to zero on entry into force.

The release did not name an entry-into-force date. Signature, legal review and ratification still have to follow, so the practical effect will arrive over time rather than at once.

How German companies read it

German business has been positioning for this for some time. A KPMG survey of 106 German companies active in India, conducted between March and May 2026 and published in June 2026, found that 96 percent expect a positive effect of the agreement on their business and 34 percent expect very significant positive effects. 52 percent plan to increase investment in India by 2026 and 80 percent by 2031.

The engineering footprint is already large. A report by the Indo-German Chamber of Commerce and ISG, covered in October 2026, counts more than 80 German companies running over 150 capability centre units in India with more than 130,000 professionals. On the Indian side, Nasscom expects the technology industry to reach about 315 billion USD of revenue in FY26, growth of 6.1 percent.

The key point: the FTA reduces political risk and eases mobility, but it does not replace the contractual, data protection and labour law structure of an engagement. A poorly structured contract does not become compliant because a trade agreement exists.

Why it matters for board conversations

Boards and investors often ask a strategic question behind the cost question: is a dependency on Indian engineering capacity a geopolitical or regulatory risk? Until 2026, the honest answer included uncertainty about future market access. The concluded agreement, together with the size of the existing German footprint in India, strengthens the case that this is a durable sourcing relationship, comparable in stability to established nearshore options. That makes it easier to justify multi-year commitments, investment in onboarding and documentation, and longer contract terms with better economics.

Nearshore comparison in a new light

DACH buyers have often favoured nearshore locations inside the EU because they share the single market, a common legal framework and free movement. India cannot match all of that, and the FTA does not try to. What it does is narrow the gap on predictability: bound services commitments and a mobility framework reduce two of the uncertainties that used to weigh against India in sourcing decisions. The remaining differences, such as time zone, data transfer requirements and travel distance, are operational and can be managed through overlap windows, documented safeguards and planned visits. The comparison therefore shifts from "stable versus uncertain" towards a more neutral trade-off of cost, scale and talent depth.

Five actions for 2027 planning

  1. Update your risk register. Reflect the concluded FTA in the assessment of India as a sourcing location, and set a review point once entry into force is clear.
  2. Plan travel deliberately. Build regular in-person workshops, onboarding visits and acceptance meetings into project plans, and track how mobility rules evolve.
  3. Keep the contract structure clean. On-site visits by an external team should support defined deliverables, not turn into integrated staffing directed by your managers.
  4. Review data transfer documentation. Standardvertragsklauseln and transfer impact assessments remain required; check that they are current.
  5. Revisit contract terms. Lower strategic risk can justify longer terms in exchange for price stability and continuity commitments.
