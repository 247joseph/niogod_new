Mittelstand Without a Software Department: Five Ways to Staff Digital Projects

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

Many Mittelstand companies in Germany, Austria and Switzerland now need software they cannot buy off the shelf: a customer portal for spare parts, a connector between machines and the ERP, an AI pilot that has to reach production. Most of them have a capable IT department that runs infrastructure and business applications, but no team that designs, builds and maintains custom software. Hiring one from scratch is slow, and leaving the gap open is expensive. The right staffing model depends on how strategic the software is, how long it will live, and how much management attention the company can spare. There are five realistic options, and each fits a different situation.

Where Mittelstand digitalisation stands

KfW Research's April 2026 analysis of the KfW-Mittelstandspanel shows that digitalisation in German SMEs has lost momentum. Only 30 percent of Mittelstand companies completed digitalisation projects in the period 2022 to 2024, five percentage points fewer than in 2021 to 2023. Total digitalisation spending fell to EUR 23,8 billion, a decline of EUR 8,1 billion. The gap between leaders and laggards is extreme: the top quarter of Mittelstand firms holds an average of EUR 156.600 in digital capital, while the bottom half averages less than EUR 50.

At the same time pressure is rising. Bitkom reported in September 2026 that 57 percent of German companies now use artificial intelligence, up from 20 percent in 2024. Customers, OEMs and regulators expect data interfaces, digital product information and connected services. For a Mittelstand firm without its own developers, the question is no longer whether to build software, but who builds it.

How Mittelstand firms use outside expertise today

A November 2026 KfW Research study on external knowledge in digitalisation found that 15 percent of digitally active Mittelstand firms rate specialised service providers, such as R&D, consulting and technical service firms, as a highly important source of know-how. Use rises with company size. About 40 percent of firms rate none of the external knowledge sources as highly important, which suggests that many companies run digital projects largely on internal resources, often the IT department in addition to its daily operations.

The five staffing models

ModelBest fitMain risk
1. Build an internal software teamSoftware becomes a core product feature for ten years or moreSlow ramp-up, recruiting competition with larger employers, single points of failure in small teams
2. Local IT service provider or agencyWell-defined projects close to standard technology, frequent on-site presenceHigher day rates, capacity limits, knowledge stays with the provider
3. Individual freelancersShort, specialised tasks with a clear endDependency on one person, status risk if freelancers are integrated like staff
4. Managed remote engineering podContinuous development over one to three years with clear scope per sprintRequires a named internal product owner and good specifications
5. Standard software with configurationProcesses that are not differentiatingAdapting processes to the tool, licence lock-in

Choosing between them: four questions

  1. Is the software part of what customers pay for? If a connected service or digital product feature differentiates your machines, you need long-term ownership of the code and the knowledge, which points to model 1 or to model 4 with a planned knowledge transfer.
  2. How long will it live? A one-off integration may suit a local provider or a freelancer. A platform that will evolve for years needs continuity that individual contractors rarely provide.
  3. Who will own it internally? Every model except standard software needs an internal product owner who decides priorities and accepts results. Without that person, no external model works well.
  4. What can the IT department absorb? Be honest about operational load. If the IT team already runs ERP, networks and security, adding software development usually means both suffer.

Common patterns that work

Three combinations appear frequently in successful Mittelstand programmes. The first pairs standard software for back-office processes with a small external development unit for the differentiating layer, such as a customer portal or machine data service. The second starts with an external team that builds and documents the first release, while the company hires one or two internal engineers who take over architecture ownership over 12 to 18 months. The third uses a local partner for on-site work such as shop floor integration and a remote team for backend and cloud development, with clear interfaces between them.

All three share one principle: the company keeps product ownership, decision rights and documentation, even where it does not keep every developer.

Budget realism

Whatever model you choose, budget for the internal side as well. A product owner typically needs a substantial part of their working time for a continuous development stream, and the IT department needs time for access, environments and integration. Projects that budget only the external cost tend to stall in the first quarter, not because the developers are slow, but because nobody internal has time to decide and accept.

A note on language and governance

Mittelstand projects fail more often on communication than on technology. Requirements come from production managers, sales engineers and quality departments who work in German and think in processes, not tickets. Whatever model you choose, make sure someone on the delivery side can take requirements in German, translate them into technical scope and report progress in a form management can read. Agree acceptance criteria per delivery, so that the company decides when work is done.

Where freelancers or external teams work closely with your staff, the contractual setup matters as well, because integration into your organisation can raise questions of Scheinselbständigkeit or Arbeitnehmerüberlassung. This is general information, not legal or tax advice.
