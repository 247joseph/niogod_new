From AI Pilot to Production: Closing the Engineering Gap in the Mittelstand

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

Artificial intelligence has crossed the adoption threshold in German business. The harder step, turning pilots into production systems that are maintained, monitored and integrated into core processes, is where most Mittelstand companies now stall. The bottleneck is rarely the model. It is the engineering around it: data pipelines, integration into ERP and MES, evaluation, security and operations. The way through is to treat production AI as a software engineering programme with defined ownership, rather than as a series of experiments run by enthusiasts in the business units.

Adoption has outrun capability

Bitkom's 2026 survey of 603 companies with 20 or more employees, published in September 2026, found that 57 percent now use AI, up from 36 percent in 2025 and 20 percent in 2024. Another 38 percent are planning or discussing it. Only 11 percent already use AI agents, while 29 percent plan to.

The same survey shows the capability gap. Among companies not yet using AI, 85 percent cite a lack of technical implementation know-how and 51 percent cite missing personnel. Across all companies, 66 percent rate their employees' AI competence as low. KfW Research adds the Mittelstand view: 20 percent of small and medium-sized firms used AI in 2022 to 2024, with firms of 50 or more employees at 36 percent, and KfW names missing skilled workers and competencies as a leading barrier.

Put simply: interest and budgets exist, but the engineering capacity to take AI beyond a pilot is thin, especially in companies without an established software team.

Why pilots stall

  • The pilot was built outside the IT landscape. A proof of concept on exported spreadsheets does not survive contact with live ERP data, access rights and change management.
  • Nobody owns operations. Models drift, data sources change, prompts and retrieval indexes need maintenance. Without an owner, quality degrades silently.
  • No evaluation baseline. Without a defined test set and acceptance threshold, nobody can say whether version two is better than version one.
  • Compliance questions arrive late. Data protection, works council involvement and EU AI Act classification surface after the pilot, and stop rollout.

A six-step path to production

1. Select by process value, not by novelty

Choose use cases where a measurable process metric improves: quote turnaround time, first-pass yield, service ticket handling time. If you cannot name the metric, the use case is not ready for production investment.

2. Define the production contract

Write down what "in production" means: data sources, latency, availability, accuracy threshold on a fixed evaluation set, human review steps, and who is called when it fails. This document becomes the acceptance basis for every later release.

3. Build the integration first

Connect to the real systems early, with real permissions. Integration with SAP, MES or a document management system is usually the largest share of effort and the main reason pilots die.

4. Establish evaluation and monitoring

Maintain a versioned test set, run it automatically on every change, and monitor live outputs for drift. This is ordinary software quality practice applied to a probabilistic component.

5. Settle compliance in parallel

Run data protection assessment, works council information and AI Act classification alongside development rather than after it. Most Mittelstand use cases are not high risk under the AI Act, but the classification should be documented.

6. Assign long-term ownership

Decide who maintains the system after launch. If the answer is "the team that built the pilot, alongside their day job", the system will decay.

Staffing the gap: three options compared

OptionStrengthLimitation
Hire an internal AI teamLong-term ownership, domain knowledge builds upSlow to staff, and AI engineers with production experience remain scarce
Upskill existing IT staffRetains process knowledge, supports acceptanceTakes time, and existing staff are already loaded with operations
External delivery team with handoverFast start, brings production patternsRequires clear specifications and planned knowledge transfer to avoid dependency

In practice, the most robust approach combines all three: an internal product owner who knows the process, an external team that builds integration and operations tooling to a defined specification, and a training plan so that internal staff can take over parts of operations over time. Bitkom reports that 70 percent of companies already offer AI training, so the upskilling component often exists and only needs to be linked to a real production system.

Budgeting beyond the pilot

A pilot budget typically covers a few weeks of experimentation. A production system needs a different budget structure: one-off effort for integration, evaluation tooling and security review, followed by recurring cost for monitoring, model and prompt maintenance, infrastructure and inference. Bitkom reports that 45 percent of companies are spending more on AI in 2026 than in the prior year, yet 63 percent of non-users cite hard-to-calculate costs as a barrier. A simple rule helps: plan the first year of operations at the same time as the build, and present both to the steering committee as one decision. A use case that only pays off without operating costs does not pay off.

Checklist for the steering committee

  • Every AI use case has a named business owner and a measurable target metric.
  • A written production contract exists before development starts.
  • Integration with core systems is in scope from the first sprint.
  • An evaluation set and acceptance threshold are versioned and automated.
  • Data protection, works council and AI Act classification are tracked as workstreams.
  • Operations ownership after launch is assigned and budgeted.
