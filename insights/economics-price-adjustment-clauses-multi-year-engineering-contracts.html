Price Adjustment Clauses in Multi-Year Engineering Contracts and the PrKG

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

Multi-year engineering contracts need an answer to one question that is often left to the renewal negotiation: how does the price change over time? Without a clause, the vendor asks for an increase at a moment of its choosing, typically when switching costs are highest. With a badly drafted clause, the buyer may be bound to an index that has nothing to do with the work, or the clause may conflict with the Preisklauselgesetz (PrKG). The answer is a review clause with a defined date, a defined cost reference and a cap, drafted in a form the PrKG allows.

Why the question matters now

The cost drivers on the two sides of a German and Indian engineering contract move at different speeds. In Germany, Destatis reported nominal wage growth of 4.1 percent in the second quarter of 2026 against consumer price inflation of 2.5 percent, and its flash estimate put inflation at 2.8 percent in October 2026. In India, Aon's salary survey projected average increases of 9.1 percent for 2026, and its latest study projects 9.1 percent again for 2027. Attrition remains a cost factor too: Wipro reported trailing twelve-month voluntary attrition of 13.8 percent for the quarter to September 2026.

A vendor whose main cost rises by around nine percent a year will not hold a fixed price for three years without either a margin buffer up front or a mechanism to adjust later. The buyer's choice is between paying for that buffer on day one and agreeing a transparent mechanism.

What the Preisklauselgesetz permits

The PrKG starts from a prohibition. Under §1 Abs. 1 PrKG, the amount of a money debt may not be determined directly and automatically by the price or value of goods or services that are not comparable with the contractual performance. The statute then lists exceptions in §1 Abs. 2 PrKG:

  • Leistungsvorbehalt. The parties agree to renegotiate the price when a reference value changes, with discretion in the outcome. No automatic adjustment, therefore outside the prohibition.
  • Spannungsklausel. The price is linked to the price of comparable goods or services, for example market rates for similar engineering services.
  • Kostenelementeklausel. The price is linked to the creditor's own cost elements, to the extent they directly affect the cost of providing the service. Salary costs of the delivery team are the obvious candidate.
  • Clauses that can only lead to a reduction.

General inflation indexation via the consumer price index is permitted under §3 PrKG only for long-term contracts, for example those with a term of at least ten years, and only if the clause is sufficiently specific and does not unreasonably disadvantage one party. An engineering services contract rarely qualifies. Under §8 PrKG, an impermissible clause remains effective until a court finally declares it invalid, but this is cold comfort if the clause is also standard business terms and fails AGB review.

A recommended review clause

The following is a pattern, not drafted contract language. It should be adapted by counsel.

The monthly fee is fixed for the first twelve months. Thereafter, either party may request a review of the fee once per contract year, effective from the anniversary of the start date. The requesting party shall substantiate the request with reference to documented changes in the delivery team's personnel costs. Any increase shall not exceed [x] percent per contract year. If no agreement is reached within 30 days, the existing fee continues to apply, and either party may terminate with [y] months' notice.

Three points make this work. First, the date is fixed, so the vendor cannot open a negotiation in the middle of a critical release. Second, the cap gives the buyer a budget ceiling. Third, the fallback is the existing fee plus an orderly exit, so neither side is forced into an outcome.

Checklist before signing a multi-year contract

  1. Is it clear when the price may change, and how often?
  2. Is any adjustment linked to a cost reference that relates to the work, rather than a general index?
  3. Is there a cap per year, and is it consistent with your budget planning?
  4. Does the clause also allow a reduction if the reference moves down?
  5. What happens if the parties do not agree: does the old price continue, and is there an exit route?
  6. Has the clause been checked against the PrKG and, if it is standard business terms, against §307 BGB?

The admissibility of price clauses depends on the specific wording and the contract as a whole. This article is general information and not legal or tax advice.

Predictable pricing over several years is part of what makes an external team plannable. niogod™ works with a fixed monthly fee in EUR under a Werkvertrag and prefers a dated, capped annual review over open-ended renegotiation, so that price discussions happen once a year and never in the middle of a sprint. If you are preparing a multi-year engagement, agreeing this mechanism at the start is the conversation worth having.
