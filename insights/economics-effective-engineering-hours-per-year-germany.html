Effective Engineering Hours per Year in Germany: A Capacity Calculation

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

Engineering capacity plans in Germany are often built on a simple assumption: one full-time engineer equals one year of output. That assumption ignores how much of the contractual year is never available for delivery. Statutory and contractual leave, public holidays, sickness, training and internal coordination together remove a third or more of nominal working time. The result is roadmaps that slip without anyone underperforming, and cost comparisons with external capacity that use the wrong denominator. This guide shows how to calculate effective engineering hours per year with current German data, and how to use the figure in budgets and vendor comparisons.

Start from the calendar, not the contract

A five-day week yields 261 weekdays in 2027. That is the gross pool from which every deduction is taken. Multiplied by eight hours, it gives the 2,088 hours that many spreadsheets silently treat as available capacity. None of the following deductions is unusual. Together they are substantial.

The deductions

Annual leave

The Bundesurlaubsgesetz guarantees a minimum of 20 days of paid leave on a five-day week. In practice, the norm is higher. The WSI collective agreement archive of the Hans-Böckler-Stiftung, in its June 2026 update, describes 30 days of paid leave per year as the standard in most collective agreements. Technology companies outside collective agreements usually offer 28 to 30 days to compete for engineers.

Public holidays

Germany has nine nationwide public holidays, and the number rises to 12 in Baden-Württemberg and 13 in parts of Bavaria, with additional local holidays in some places. How many fall on weekdays changes each year. A team distributed across several states, or across Germany, Austria and Switzerland, has a different holiday calendar for each member.

Sickness

DAK-Gesundheit reported an average of 19.5 days of incapacity to work per insured employee in 2025, slightly below 19.7 days in 2024, at a sickness rate of 5.4 percent. These are calendar days, so the working days lost are lower. For the first half of 2026, DAK reported a slightly lower sickness rate of 5.3 percent. Office-based technical roles typically sit below the all-employee average, but no plan should assume zero.

Training and internal events

Conferences, certifications, company offsites and onboarding of new colleagues are legitimate uses of time, and a healthy engineering culture needs them. They are still not delivery time.

Coordination overhead

Stand-ups, planning, refinement, reviews, retrospectives, one-to-ones, incident follow-ups and ad hoc questions consume a share of every remaining day. The share rises with team size and with the number of stakeholders an engineer serves.

In this example, focused delivery time is 59 percent of the nominal 2,088 hours. If the fully loaded annual cost of a senior engineer is 110,850 EUR, the cost per available hour is about 68 EUR and the cost per focused delivery hour about 91 EUR. Both figures are illustrative, but the ratio between them is the point: every comparison that divides annual cost by 2,088 understates the true hourly cost of internal capacity by roughly a third or more.

Where the number is used

Roadmap planning

A quarter has roughly 51 available days per engineer under these assumptions, not 65. Plans that commit 65 days of work per person per quarter build in a structural overrun of more than 25 percent. The first quarter and the summer months are typically lower again because of holiday clustering.

Cost comparisons

External offers are usually quoted per billed day or as a fixed monthly fee. Billed days are by definition available days, since the provider absorbs its own leave and sickness. Comparing a day rate with an internal salary divided by 261 days compares two different units. The correct internal denominator is available days, and ideally focused hours.

Continuity

Leave and sickness do not only reduce capacity, they interrupt it. A team of three loses its only backend specialist for two weeks in August, and the sprint stalls. Plans should identify single points of failure and decide whether to accept the risk, cross-train, or contract for continuity.

Part-time and parental leave

Part-time arrangements, parental leave and sabbaticals are another factor that capacity plans often miss. In engineering teams with many people between 30 and 45 they are a plannable normal case, not an exception. Expressing team capacity in full-time equivalents rather than heads, and moving foreseeable absences into quarterly planning early, avoids surprises in the middle of a release cycle.

A checklist for your own calculation

  • Take weekdays for the plan year from the calendar, not 52 times five.
  • Use the actual leave entitlement in your contracts, including any additional days for tenure.
  • Use the holiday calendar of each team member's place of work.
  • Take your own sickness data from payroll, averaged over at least two years.
  • Measure coordination time once, for example over two sprints, instead of estimating it.
  • Recalculate when the team grows, because coordination overhead does not scale linearly.

What this means for external capacity

Effective hours are not an argument for working more. German leave entitlements and sickness rates are part of a working model that keeps experienced engineers productive over decades. They are an argument for planning with the right denominator, and for knowing which capacity is contractually guaranteed and which is subject to the normal variation of a team.
