IEC 81001-5-1 Security Lifecycle When an External Team Writes the Code

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

Medical device software now has to show a documented security lifecycle, not just secure code. For manufacturers in Germany, Austria and Switzerland, IEC 81001-5-1 has become the reference framework for that lifecycle, sitting next to IEC 62304 and translating the MDR's general requirements on IT security into concrete activities. When an external team writes part of the software, the question is not whether those activities can be outsourced, but how they are divided, who produces which evidence, and how the manufacturer keeps control. The short answer: the external team can produce most of the evidence, but the manufacturer must own the security risk decisions, the threat model sign-off and the post-market vulnerability process.

The regulatory and market context

Annex I of the MDR requires software to be developed according to the state of the art, including information security, and to set minimum IT security measures. The MDCG 2019-16 guidance on cybersecurity for medical devices elaborates these expectations. IEC 81001-5-1, published in 2021, structures them into a health software security lifecycle covering planning, requirements, architecture, implementation, verification, release, maintenance and problem resolution. Its status as a harmonised standard under the MDR has been on the Commission's agenda for some time; manufacturers should check the current listing in the Official Journal rather than rely on older timelines.

The pressure on manufacturers is real. BVMed's market overview from November 2026 puts German medtech revenue at 42,7 billion EUR, with an export share of about 68 percent and over 210.000 employees, and notes that 93 percent of companies have fewer than 250 employees. Many of these SMEs are still working through MDR recertification: under Regulation (EU) 2023/607, legacy devices of class III and implantable class IIb may stay on the market until 31 December 2027 and other classes until 31 December 2028, subject to conditions. Security engineering competes with that workload for the same small teams. Bitkom's September 2026 study found 11 percent of companies with IT vacancies searching for security profiles, which are among the hardest to fill.

Two principles follow from the standard itself. First, testing independence: the people who test security should not be the same as those who wrote the code, which is a natural place to use a separate provider or an internal team. Second, supplier control: the manufacturer must evaluate suppliers critically, because weak supplier security is a common attack path. An external development team is a supplier under the QMS, with an agreement that names its security duties.

Making it contractual

The supplier agreement and the sprint-level Leistungsbeschreibung are where these responsibilities become enforceable. A workable pattern includes:

  • A security clause referencing IEC 81001-5-1 activities in scope, with deliverables per activity.
  • A definition of done that includes updated threat model entries, SBOM delta and static analysis results for every sprint.
  • Response times for vulnerability analysis, for example an initial assessment within two working days of notification, as an illustrative value to be agreed.
  • Access controls for the development environment, code signing keys held by the manufacturer only.
  • Audit rights and a procedure for the manufacturer's quality assurance to review records.
  • Acceptance (Abnahme) of each sprint deliverable against these criteria, documented in the design history.

The vulnerability process cannot be outsourced

After release, devices in the field depend on the manufacturer's ability to detect and act on vulnerabilities. The external team can monitor component advisories against the SBOM and prepare analyses, but the decision whether a vulnerability creates an unacceptable risk, whether to notify authorities, and how to communicate with hospitals stays with the manufacturer. Note that medical devices under the MDR are generally excluded from the EU Cyber Resilience Act, so the MDR vigilance system and MDCG guidance remain the main framework; accessories or non-device software in the same product family may be treated differently.

What auditors will look for

When a notified body or an internal auditor reviews the security lifecycle, the question is rarely whether a single activity was done. It is whether the chain is complete and traceable: from a threat in the model to a security requirement, from the requirement to a design decision and a test, and from a field vulnerability back to the affected components and versions. With an external team, the weak point is usually the hand-over between the supplier's tools and the manufacturer's QMS. Agree early on one requirements and traceability tool, or a defined export, so that evidence produced remotely lands in the design history file without manual copying.

Checklist before onboarding an external team

  1. Security plan updated to name the supplier and its scope.
  2. Supplier evaluation completed, including security practices and access controls.
  3. Threat model template and review procedure defined.
  4. SBOM format and tooling agreed.
  5. Independent security testing arranged separately from the development supplier.
  6. Vulnerability handling procedure with response times agreed.
  7. Signing keys and release authority held by the manufacturer.

This article is general information and not legal or regulatory advice; the applicable harmonised standards and the scope of the MDR and CRA should be confirmed with your regulatory affairs function or notified body.
