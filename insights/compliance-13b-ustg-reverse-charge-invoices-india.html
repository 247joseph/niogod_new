§13b UStG Reverse Charge on Invoices from Indian Software Vendors

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

When a German company buys software development services from a vendor based in India, the invoice should not show German VAT. That does not mean the service is tax-free. Under the reverse charge mechanism of §13b UStG, the German business customer owes the VAT itself and, in most cases, deducts it as input tax in the same return. For fully taxable businesses the net cash effect is usually zero. For businesses with VAT-exempt revenues, such as many healthcare providers, it can become a real cost. And invoices drafted incorrectly can create a double burden. This briefing sets out the mechanics and a short invoice check.

The numbers behind the invoice

Cross-border engineering invoices are no longer a niche case in German accounting. Germany Trade & Invest (GTAI) calculated that trade between Germany and India reached a record EUR 31.3 billion in 2025. On 27 January 2026 the EU and India concluded negotiations on their free trade agreement, and the European Commission puts annual EU-India trade in goods and services at more than EUR 180 billion. On the supply side, NASSCOM estimates India's technology industry revenue at USD 315 billion for FY26, of which USD 63 billion comes from engineering, research and development services.

Tax administrations watch these flows closely. In its 2025 VAT gap report, the European Commission estimated the EU-wide VAT compliance gap for 2023 at EUR 128 billion, or 9.5 percent of the theoretical VAT liability, with Germany at 9.7 percent. The reverse charge mechanism is one of the tools used to secure collection on cross-border supplies, which is one reason why auditors look carefully at §13b entries even when the net cash effect is zero.

Step one: place of supply is Germany

Software development, maintenance and consulting are sonstige Leistungen (services). For services between businesses, §3a Abs. 2 UStG places the supply where the recipient operates its business. A German company receiving services from India therefore receives a supply that is taxable in Germany.

Step two: the recipient owes the tax

§13b Abs. 2 Nr. 1 UStG covers services of an entrepreneur established abroad (im Ausland ansässiger Unternehmer). Under §13b Abs. 7 UStG, that is an entrepreneur with no residence, seat, management or permanent establishment in Germany. §13b Abs. 5 Satz 1 UStG makes the recipient liable for the tax if it is an entrepreneur or a legal entity. The Indian vendor does not register for German VAT for this supply and does not charge it.

Step three: when the tax arises

SituationRule
Supplier established in a third country such as India (§13b Abs. 2 Nr. 1 UStG)Tax arises on issue of the invoice, at the latest at the end of the calendar month following the month in which the service was performed
Supplier established in another EU member state (§13b Abs. 1 UStG)Tax arises at the end of the VAT return period in which the service was performed
Payment made before the service is performed (§13b Abs. 4 Satz 2 UStG)Tax arises at the end of the return period in which the payment was made, to the extent of the payment

For a monthly fee arrangement, it helps to agree in the contract that each month is a separately invoiced partial service. That gives a clear performance date per invoice and avoids questions about long-running services.

Step four: input tax deduction

Under §15 Abs. 1 Satz 1 Nr. 4 UStG, the recipient can deduct the tax it owes under §13b as input tax, provided the service is used for transactions that entitle it to deduct. Output tax and input tax are reported in the same VAT return. For a fully taxable software company, the result is cash neutral.

The picture changes for businesses with VAT-exempt revenues. Medical practices, clinics, certain healthcare and education providers, insurers and financial institutions often have partially or fully excluded input tax deduction under §15 Abs. 2 UStG. For them, the German VAT on offshore services is a real cost that belongs in the business case.

Illustrative calculation

Assume a monthly fee of EUR 10,000 net (an illustrative figure, not a price quote). The recipient owes 19 percent German VAT, EUR 1,900, under §13b.

  • Fully taxable recipient: deducts EUR 1,900 as input tax. Net VAT cost: EUR 0.
  • Recipient with 30 percent of revenues VAT-exempt and a corresponding pro-rata deduction (illustrative assumption): deducts EUR 1,330. Net VAT cost: EUR 570 per month.
  • Fully exempt recipient: no deduction. Net VAT cost: EUR 1,900 per month.

The invoice check

  1. No German VAT shown. If an Indian vendor shows German VAT on its invoice, that amount is generally owed by the vendor as incorrectly shown tax under §14c UStG, is not deductible for you as input tax, and does not relieve you of your own §13b liability. Ask for a corrected invoice.
  2. Reference to the reverse charge. §14a Abs. 5 UStG requires the note Steuerschuldnerschaft des Leistungsempfängers. Its absence does not shift the liability back to the vendor, but a complete invoice simplifies audits.
  3. Complete standard details. Names and addresses, date, invoice number, description of the service, performance period, net amount.
  4. Indian taxes. Indian vendors typically treat services for foreign customers as exports. If Indian GST appears on the invoice, clarify the reason with the vendor.
  5. Currency. If the invoice is in a foreign currency, the conversion for VAT purposes follows §16 Abs. 6 UStG, generally using the monthly average rates published by the Federal Ministry of Finance.

Typical bookkeeping errors

  • The invoice is booked as a net invoice and not reported in the VAT return at all, because no tax amount is shown.
  • A late invoice delays reporting, although the tax arose at the latest at the end of the month following performance.
  • The service is treated like a supply from an EU member state, with a different timing rule and incorrect reporting.
  • With pro-rata deduction, the non-deductible part is not recorded as a cost.

Adjacent questions

Withholding tax

Payments for services are generally not subject to German withholding tax. Where the contract contains licence or rights components, §50a EStG can become relevant, subject to the double taxation agreement between Germany and India. This is a question for your tax adviser before the first payment, not after an audit.

Austria and Switzerland

Austria has a comparable transfer of tax liability for services from foreign entrepreneurs under §19 Abs. 1 UStG 1994. Switzerland applies the acquisition tax (Bezugsteuer) under Art. 45 MWSTG for services from companies abroad. The principles are similar, details and thresholds differ.

Contract points that help accounting

  • Monthly partial services with defined performance periods
  • Invoicing within a fixed number of days after month end
  • Invoice currency and the vendor's legal name and address as in the contract
  • Separate line items for services and any rights or licence components
