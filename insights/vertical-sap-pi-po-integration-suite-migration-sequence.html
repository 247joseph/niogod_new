SAP PI/PO to Integration Suite: A Migration Sequence Before 2027

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

SAP Process Integration and Process Orchestration (PI/PO) sits quietly at the centre of many DACH SAP landscapes, connecting ERP to suppliers, banks, logistics providers, MES and customer portals. Its mainstream maintenance ends with the same 2027 horizon as ECC, yet it receives a fraction of the attention. The answer is to treat the move to SAP Integration Suite as a separate, interface-by-interface migration with its own inventory, test harness and cutover waves, run ahead of or in parallel with the S/4HANA programme rather than squeezed into it.

The timeline

Under SAP's published maintenance timeline, mainstream maintenance for PI/PO on SAP NetWeaver 7.5 ends at the end of 2027, with optional extended maintenance to the end of 2030. That mirrors the ECC dates: mainstream maintenance to the end of 2027 and extended maintenance to the end of 2030 at an additional premium, as The Register reported in February 2026. The overlap is the problem. A DSAG survey of 198 senior leaders in Germany, Austria and Switzerland, conducted between December 2025 and January 2026, found that only 37 percent of ECC users planned to complete their S/4HANA move by the end of 2027. Most companies will therefore be migrating ERP and middleware in the same window, competing for the same scarce SAP specialists.

Execution risk is already visible. ISG research among 200 senior decision-makers, reported in February 2026, found that nearly 60 percent of SAP migration projects were delayed and over budget. And the specialists are hard to find: Bitkom counted 79,000 unfilled IT positions in Germany in September 2026, with 97 percent of companies with vacancies reporting difficulty filling them.

Why integration deserves its own track

  • Different risk profile. An interface failure stops a supplier, a bank file or a production order immediately. ERP issues are often visible internally first; integration issues are visible to partners first.
  • Many external parties. Each partner has its own test environment, contact and change window. Coordination, not coding, often dominates the timeline.
  • High repeatability. Many interfaces follow a small number of patterns. Once a pattern is migrated and tested, the next instances are much faster.
  • Independent value. Moving integration first decouples the S/4HANA cutover from partner connectivity, which reduces risk on go-live weekend.

A six-step migration sequence

  1. Inventory. Export every integration scenario with sender, receiver, adapter, mapping type, message volume, error rate and business owner. Mark scenarios with no traffic in the last twelve months for retirement.
  2. Pattern classification. Group interfaces by pattern, for example IDoc to file, SOAP to REST, EDI via a value-added network, bank formats, or synchronous lookups. Patterns, not individual interfaces, become the unit of design.
  3. Target decisions per pattern. Decide whether each pattern moves as is, is rebuilt in a modern form, or is replaced by a standard connector or API. Document the decision and the reason.
  4. Test harness. Capture real message samples from PI/PO, anonymise where necessary, and build automated comparisons of output between old and new flows. This is the single most valuable asset of the project.
  5. Waves. Migrate in waves of related interfaces, starting with low-risk internal flows, then partner flows with cooperative partners, and finally critical bank and logistics interfaces.
  6. Parallel run and decommissioning. Run old and new in parallel where possible, compare outputs, switch over, and decommission PI/PO components wave by wave.

An illustrative wave plan

As an illustrative example for a mid-sized landscape with around 250 active interfaces: wave one covers 40 internal, low-volume flows and serves to prove the patterns, the test harness and the new monitoring. Wave two moves 80 partner flows with partners that have test systems and responsive contacts. Wave three covers the remaining partner flows, and wave four the critical bank, customs and logistics interfaces, each with an extended parallel run. If each wave takes one quarter including stabilisation, the programme fits into roughly a year, which leaves a buffer before the end of 2027 only if work starts in early 2027 at the latest.

Who owns what

An internal integration owner keeps the architecture, the pattern catalogue and the relationships with partners. Business owners sign off the behaviour of their interfaces against the comparison reports. The delivery team, internal or external, owns the rebuilt flows, the test harness and the documentation of each wave. Clear ownership turns acceptance into a fact-based step rather than a negotiation.

Strategic alignment

Not every company follows SAP's roadmap to the letter. In the same DSAG survey, 35 percent said they base investment planning strongly or very strongly on SAP's vision, while 62 percent do so less strongly or not at all. That independence is legitimate, but integration architecture outlives individual products. Whatever the target platform, the inventory, pattern catalogue and test harness built during migration are reusable assets and should be owned by the company, not by a tool or a supplier.

Checklist before the first wave

  • Complete interface inventory with owners, volumes and retirement candidates
  • Pattern catalogue with target decision per pattern
  • Automated output comparison on anonymised real messages
  • Partner communication plan with test windows
  • Monitoring, alerting and runbooks for the new platform
  • Certificate and credential inventory
  • Decommissioning plan per wave

Interface migration is pattern-based, test-driven work that can be specified per wave and accepted against automated comparisons.
