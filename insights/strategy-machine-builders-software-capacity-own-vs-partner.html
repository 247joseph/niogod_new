Machine Builders as Software Companies: What to Own and What to Partner

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

German machine and plant builders compete more and more on software: connectivity, condition monitoring, user interfaces, digital services and, increasingly, AI-based features. Most of them grew up as mechanical and electrical engineering companies, and their software capacity has not grown at the same pace as customer expectations. The answer is not to build a complete software organisation for every layer of the stack. It is to decide deliberately which software capabilities differentiate the machine and must be owned, and which are necessary but generic and can be delivered by partners under clear specifications.

Where the sector stands

A VDMA study of 248 member companies, presented at Hannover Messe in April 2026, gives a mixed picture. 74 percent of machine builders see themselves at least on par with competitors in digitalisation, and about 60 percent have a digitalisation strategy. But 22 percent have no clear strategic direction, and the share with a strategy falls from 83 percent among companies with 3,000 or more employees to 53 percent among those with up to 49 employees.

The hurdles the study names are telling: IT security (60 percent), change management (47 percent), implementation speed (44 percent) and personnel resources (42 percent). Three of these four are capacity problems. The wider Mittelstand context points the same way. KfW Research reports that only 30 percent of Mittelstand firms completed a digitalisation project in 2022 to 2024, with total digitalisation spending falling to 23.8 billion EUR. And although Bitkom's 2026 study shows the overall IT shortage easing to 79,000 open positions, 97 percent of companies with IT vacancies still report difficulty filling them.

The software stack of a modern machine

To decide what to own, first make the layers explicit. A typical connected machine has five software layers.

LayerExamplesDifferentiating?Typical recommendation
1. Control and safety logicPLC code, motion control, safety functionsYes, core process know-howOwn
2. Process intelligenceParameter optimisation, quality prediction, process modelsYes, often the future differentiatorOwn the models and data; partner for tooling
3. HMI and operator softwarePanels, operator guidance, configuration toolsPartly, through usabilityOwn design and requirements; partner for implementation
4. Connectivity and integrationOPC UA servers, MES and ERP interfaces, edge gatewaysRarely, expected as standardPartner, under defined interface specifications
5. Cloud and digital servicesRemote monitoring portals, dashboards, service appsDepends on business modelOwn product management; partner for build and operations

The pattern: own the know-how that encodes how your machine performs its process, and own the product decisions around software. Source the engineering that implements standard interfaces, portals and tooling.

Four tests for each capability

  1. Differentiation test: would a customer choose your machine over a competitor's because of this capability? If not, it is a candidate for partnering.
  2. Knowledge test: does building it require deep process or mechanical knowledge that only your engineers have? If yes, keep it close.
  3. Specification test: can the work be described in a Leistungsbeschreibung with clear interfaces and acceptance criteria? If yes, it can be delivered externally.
  4. Certification test: does it fall under safety or product regulation that requires your own documented development process? If yes, any partner must work inside that process.

Common mistakes

  • Treating software as a by-product of the machine project. Software then gets staffed per order, and no reusable platform emerges.
  • Outsourcing the core. Handing process intelligence to a partner without retaining the data and models erodes the main future differentiator.
  • Insourcing everything. Trying to hire cloud, front-end and integration specialists for a team of a few dozen software engineers slows everything down, especially for smaller firms where the VDMA data shows strategy gaps are largest.
  • Ignoring security as a design input. With IT security named as the top hurdle by 60 percent of VDMA respondents, security requirements belong in every specification, including those given to partners.

Organising a mixed model

A workable structure for a mid-sized machine builder has three elements. First, a small internal software leadership that owns architecture, product management for software and the interfaces between layers. Second, internal engineers who own control logic and process intelligence. Third, one or two external teams that deliver connectivity, HMI implementation and digital services against specifications set by the internal leadership, with acceptance at defined milestones. The internal team remains the system integrator and keeps the knowledge of how everything fits together.

This structure also makes language and coordination manageable. Specifications, acceptance meetings and customer-facing discussions often need to happen in German, so the interface between internal leadership and external teams should be staffed accordingly.

Regulation raises the stakes

The EU Machinery Regulation (EU) 2023/1230 applies from 20 January 2027 and explicitly addresses software that performs safety functions and protection against corruption, including cyber threats. This strengthens the case for owning safety logic and for writing security requirements into every external specification. It also means that partners working on connected layers need to understand how their deliverables feed into the manufacturer's conformity documentation, not just how to write the code.

A first step for the next planning cycle

Map your current and planned software features onto the five layers. For each, record who builds it today, how many people it needs, and how it scores on the four tests. The result is a capacity plan that tells you which roles to hire, which to develop internally, and which work packages to tender. It also gives management a clear answer to the question of why software capacity is growing and where the money goes.
