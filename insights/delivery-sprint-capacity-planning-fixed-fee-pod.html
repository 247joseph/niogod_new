Sprint Capacity Planning for a Fixed-Fee Engineering Pod

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

A fixed monthly fee buys you a team and an outcome-oriented process, not a pool of hours to draw down. That changes how capacity planning works. Instead of counting billable time, you plan what the pod can realistically deliver per sprint, protect that capacity from erosion, and turn the results into a quarterly forecast your roadmap and your board can rely on. This guide sets out a planning method that works for a three-person pod spread across Germany and India, with a worked example.

Why capacity is the scarce resource

External capacity is usually bought because internal capacity is hard to get. Bitkom's 2026 labour market study counted about 79,000 missing IT specialists in Germany. That is down from 109,000 in 2025 and 149,000 in 2023, but 97 percent of companies with IT positions still report difficulties filling them, and 31 percent receive practically no applications for open IT roles. Once a pod is in place, using its capacity well is worth more than almost any other lever.

The supply side in India is large but not unlimited. NASSCOM reported a technology workforce of about 5.8 million people for the 2025 financial year, with net additions of around 126,000, a modest growth rate compared with earlier boom years. Experienced engineers are in demand there too, which makes continuity of a well-run pod valuable on both sides.

Planning failures are expensive. PMI's Pulse of the Profession 2018 found that 52 percent of projects experienced scope creep or uncontrolled changes. In sprint delivery, the usual cause is not bad estimation but planning at a capacity the team never actually had.

Step 1: Calculate available days

Start from calendar reality, not from headcount. For each sprint, list every pod member's working days and subtract:

  • Public holidays at each person's location. Indian and German holidays differ, and some Indian holidays vary by state.
  • Planned leave, collected at least one sprint ahead.
  • Recurring commitments outside the sprint backlog, such as on-call duty or support rotations.
  • Onboarding or knowledge transfer time if the team composition changed.

Publish the result as a simple availability table in the sprint planning page. It is the single most effective way to prevent the "why did we deliver less this sprint" conversation.

Step 2: Apply a focus factor

Not every available hour goes into backlog items. Ceremonies, reviews, refinement, coordination across time zones and unplanned questions all take time. The focus factor is the share of available time that historically turns into completed backlog work. For a new pod, start conservatively, for example at 60 to 70 percent, and adjust after three sprints based on actual completion.

Treat the focus factor as a measurement, not a target. If it falls, look for the cause: more meetings, more context switching, more support requests. Pushing it up by planning more work only produces carry-over.

Step 3: Reserve explicit buffers

BufferTypical share of sprint capacityPurpose
Change bufferAbout 10 percentAbsorbs small mid-sprint changes without swaps
Defect buffer5 to 15 percent, based on historyPost-acceptance fixes and regressions
MaintenanceOne story or about 10 percentDependency updates, test suite health, technical debt

Buffers that are not used flow into pulled-forward backlog items at the end of the sprint. Buffers that are always exceeded are a signal for the steering meeting, not a reason to cut them.

Step 4: Forecast with ranges

For the quarter, forecast in ranges based on the last three to six sprints of completed work, not on the best sprint. A simple method: take the lowest and the median completed volume of recent sprints, multiply by the number of sprints in the quarter, and adjust for known availability changes. Present the roadmap as "committed" (fits within the low end), "likely" (fits within the median) and "stretch".

This framing helps founders and CTOs explain plans to investors and boards. A forecast with a stated range and a track record is more credible than a single date.

Worked example (illustrative calculation)

Consider a three-person pod with a two-week sprint of ten working days per person.

  • Gross availability: 3 people times 10 days equals 30 person-days.
  • Minus one Indian public holiday for two engineers (2 days) and one day of leave (1 day): 27 person-days available.
  • Focus factor of 65 percent: about 17.5 person-days for backlog work.
  • Change buffer 10 percent, defect buffer 10 percent, maintenance 10 percent: about 5 person-days reserved.
  • Plannable for new stories: about 12.5 person-days.

Many teams plan this sprint as if it had 30 days. The difference of more than half is exactly the carry-over they later explain in the sprint review. If you estimate in story points instead of days, the same logic applies: plan against the observed velocity range, adjusted for availability.

What the fixed fee changes

Under a fixed monthly fee, more planned work does not cost more, so there is a temptation to overload sprints. Resist it. The fee pays for a sustainable delivery rate and for the vendor's responsibility for the result. Overloading converts that into rushed work, weaker tests and later defects. Conversely, if the pod regularly finishes early, that is information for a scope conversation, not idle capacity, because unused buffers flow back into the backlog.

The capacity model also supports the contract structure. Under a Werkvertrag with sprint-based Abnahme, the agreed sprint scope is what the vendor commits to. Planning that scope against realistic capacity keeps acceptance clean and avoids disputes about partial delivery.

Checklist for the next planning session

  • Availability table with holidays and leave for both locations.
  • Focus factor based on the last three sprints.
  • Change, defect and maintenance buffers visible in the plan.
  • Sprint goal stated in one sentence and agreed by the product owner.
  • Quarterly forecast updated with low, median and stretch ranges.
