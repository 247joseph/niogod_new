Industrial Edge Applications: Who Builds, Ships and Operates Them

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

Industrial edge platforms have made it easy to run containerised software next to machines: condition monitoring, energy analytics, quality models, protocol gateways. What they have not made easy is the question that follows the first successful pilot: who maintains these applications for the next eight years, across dozens of lines and several plants, with security updates and new machine variants arriving continuously? For most Mittelstand manufacturers the answer is a split model. The plant operates, a central team owns the platform and release process, and a remote engineering team builds and maintains the applications under a defined software supply chain.

The adoption picture

Edge computing has moved from experiment to infrastructure in German manufacturing. Bitkom's Industrie 4.0 survey of 552 manufacturers, published in March 2025, found that 28 percent already use edge computing and 46 percent use IoT platforms. The April 2026 edition, with 555 respondents, reported that 40 percent use AI in production and a further 38 percent plan to, and AI inference near the machine is one of the main reasons edge nodes are installed.

The regulatory environment is tightening in parallel. Under the EU Cyber Resilience Act, manufacturers' reporting obligations for actively exploited vulnerabilities and severe incidents apply from 11 September 2026, with an early warning due within 24 hours, and the main obligations apply from 11 December 2027, according to the European Commission. Edge applications that are shipped to customers as part of a machine or as a software product can fall into scope, which turns release and vulnerability handling into a compliance topic, not only an operational one.

Staff are the limiting factor. Bitkom's September 2026 labour market study found that 14 percent of companies with IT vacancies are looking for cloud and infrastructure profiles and 17 percent for software development and architecture. Engineers who combine container orchestration with an understanding of PLCs and fieldbuses are rare in any single hiring market.

Three roles, three owners

RoleResponsibilitiesTypical owner
BuildApplication code, connectors to OPC UA or other protocols, tests, container images, documentationRemote engineering team
ShipSigned images, software bill of materials, vulnerability scanning, release notes, staged rollout rulesCentral platform team, with the remote team preparing artefacts
OperateDevice fleet health, rollout approvals per line, local troubleshooting, change windowsPlant IT and OT, supported by the central team

The critical boundary is between build and ship. If the remote team can push directly to devices, the plant loses control; if every release depends on a manual step by one central engineer, updates stall. A pipeline in which the remote team produces signed, scanned artefacts and the central team approves promotion through stages resolves this.

A minimum software supply chain

  1. Single source repository per application, with protected main branch and mandatory review.
  2. Reproducible builds producing container images tagged with the commit hash.
  3. Software bill of materials generated on every build, stored with the image.
  4. Vulnerability scanning on build and periodically on deployed images, with a triage rule for severity and exploitability.
  5. Signing of images, with devices configured to run signed images only.
  6. Staged rollout: test cell, one production line, one plant, all plants, each with an approval and a rollback path.
  7. Telemetry from every node: version running, resource use, connector health.

Each step yields evidence that can serve as acceptance criteria in a sprint-based Leistungsbeschreibung. A sprint is accepted when its release has passed the pipeline, the SBOM is complete and no unaccepted critical vulnerability remains.

Testing without the machine

Remote teams cannot stand next to the press or the injection moulding machine, so the test strategy must bring the machine to them. Recorded OPC UA traffic, simulated servers that replay realistic value ranges, and a small physical test cell at the central site cover most cases. The plant then only needs to validate behaviour in the first production line during a staged rollout, rather than act as the primary test environment.

Operating model after go-live

Plan for a steady state in which the remote team handles application maintenance, dependency updates and new connector variants in regular sprints, plus a defined response path for vulnerabilities. Under the Cyber Resilience Act timelines, the manufacturer needs to know within hours whether a reported vulnerability affects deployed versions. The SBOM and fleet telemetry make that a query rather than an investigation. The plant retains the decision when to roll out, and the central team retains the decision what may be rolled out.

Costs that appear after the pilot

Pilots hide maintenance costs because they run on one line with one hardware revision and one machine type. At fleet scale, every new controller firmware, every additional protocol variant and every base image update creates work. An illustrative planning rule: reserve a fixed share of each sprint, for example a quarter of capacity, for dependency updates, vulnerability fixes and connector maintenance, and make that share visible in the steering committee. Treating maintenance as a budget line rather than as interruptions keeps feature work predictable and avoids the pattern in which security updates wait until the next big release.

Questions to settle before scaling beyond the pilot

  • Who is the manufacturer in the regulatory sense for each edge application, your company or a platform vendor?
  • Which support period will you commit to for each application, and who funds maintenance during it?
  • Who may approve a rollout to a production line, and within which change windows?
  • How fast must a critical vulnerability be assessed, fixed and rolled out, and who is on call?
  • Where do logs and telemetry reside, and does any of it contain personal data?

The remarks on the Cyber Resilience Act are general information and not legal advice; scope and manufacturer role should be assessed with your legal advisers.
