India's DPDP Act and 2025 Rules: What German Data Controllers Need to Know

JJ
Joseph Jose
Founder & CEO, niogod™

German companies that send personal data to an engineering team in India usually think about one regime: the GDPR and its transfer rules. Since November 2025, a second regime is phasing in at the other end. India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 (DPDP Act), made operational by the DPDP Rules 2025, imposes its own duties on whoever decides how personal data is processed in India, and its core obligations apply from around May 2027. For a German controller, the practical answer is to understand where the two regimes overlap, where they diverge and what the Indian vendor must now be able to prove under both.

Where the DPDP regime stands

The Indian government notified the DPDP Rules on 14 November 2025, as reported by BDO India and India Briefing. Implementation is staggered:

  • Immediately: establishment of the Data Protection Board of India and selected provisions of the Act.
  • After 12 months (around November 2026): rules on registration of consent managers.
  • After 18 months (around May 2027): the core compliance obligations, including notice and consent, security safeguards, breach notification, rights of data principals and transfer conditions.

Penalties under the Act can reach 250 crore rupees (about 2,5 billion rupees) per breach, according to India Briefing, with the amount depending on the type and severity of the violation. Failure to take reasonable security safeguards is among the triggers for the highest tier.

How the DPDP Act applies to work for a German client

The DPDP Act distinguishes between the Data Fiduciary, which determines purpose and means (comparable to a GDPR controller), and the Data Processor, which processes on its behalf. The Act applies to processing of digital personal data within India, and also to processing outside India if it relates to offering goods or services to people in India.

In a typical arrangement, a German company is the controller under the GDPR and instructs an Indian engineering vendor that accesses personal data, for example in a staging database or during support. The Indian vendor then acts as a processor. Section 17(1)(d) of the Act matters most here: where an India-based person processes personal data of people outside India under a contract with a person outside India, most of Chapter II, all of Chapter III and Section 16 do not apply. Two provisions remain: Section 8(1), under which the fiduciary stays responsible for compliance, and Section 8(5), the duty to protect personal data with reasonable security safeguards. In other words, for the data of your German customers the Indian regime shrinks to a security obligation, while the vendor's own Indian data (for example its employees) falls under the full regime. Whether the exemption applies to a specific arrangement, for instance where Indian residents' data is mixed in, is a question for Indian counsel.

Under the Section 17(1)(d) exemption, the rows that remain relevant for EU data processed under your contract are mainly security and its implementing rules. The others describe the regime your vendor must run for Indian data, which is still a useful indicator of its overall maturity.

The one-year log retention requirement deserves attention, because it sits in the security safeguards of the Rules. Under the GDPR, logs containing personal data must not be kept longer than necessary. Under the DPDP Rules, certain logs must be kept for at least a year. A German controller should agree with its Indian vendor which logs are retained, where, for how long and with which access controls, and reflect this in the transfer impact assessment.

Why the Indian regime helps the transfer assessment

A transfer impact assessment under the GDPR asks whether the law of the recipient country undermines the protection offered by the standard contractual clauses. The DPDP Act does not create adequacy, and questions about government access to data remain part of the assessment. But a binding domestic data protection law with security obligations, breach notification and an enforcement body is a relevant factor that controllers can now document. The context matters: according to Nasscom, India's technology industry was projected to reach about 315 billion USD in revenue in FY26 with about 5,95 million direct employees, which makes the quality of data protection practice at Indian vendors a significant question for European buyers.

A checklist for German controllers

  1. Map the data. Identify which personal data the Indian team can access at all. In many engineering engagements, the right answer is synthetic or pseudonymised data, which reduces both GDPR and DPDP exposure.
  2. Update the contract stack. Keep the Art. 28 GDPR agreement and the standard contractual clauses, and add DPDP-specific clauses on security safeguards, breach cooperation and log retention.
  3. Align breach processes. Define a single incident process that meets the 72-hour GDPR deadline and any Indian notification duties that apply, with named contacts on both sides.
  4. Revise the transfer impact assessment. Document the DPDP regime and its phase-in, and review it again once the core obligations apply in 2027.
  5. Ask for evidence. Request the vendor's DPDP readiness plan, including whether it may be designated a Significant Data Fiduciary for any of its own activities.
